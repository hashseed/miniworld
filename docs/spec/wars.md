# Wars and battles

The first list of major wars, followed by the battles and sieges shown as their own events, in chronological order. Wars are events of type "War (range)" and battles of type "Battle" ([spec.md section 5](spec.md#5-how-events-appear-in-the-world)); they sit on the timeline together with the other events in [events.md](events.md), which no longer lists wars or battles. Each war and battle has an English and a German title. Dates come from general historical knowledge, are approximate where marked "c.", and are checked against sources during the data build.

**How wars were chosen.** A war is on the list if at least one of these is true: it made, ended or reshaped an empire or a country; it changed how a large share of the world's people lived, what they believed or who ruled them; it is among the deadliest in its region or age; or it is widely taught in English- or German-speaking schools. The list is spread across the world and across time. Wars of conquest and colonial wars are listed as wars, with both sides named, and not described only from the side of the winner.

**How they are presented.** The rules of [spec.md section 4](spec.md#4-representing-history) apply. On the globe a war shows as armies in era-typical gear marching along their real routes, front lines on the border overlay, besieged towns and smoke over burning places; fallen figures sink down and fade out, and people are shown fleeing, sheltering and rebuilding, with nothing graphic. The card says who fought whom, why, how it ended and what changed, in plain words for a reader of 11 to 12. It gives the number of deaths where historians have one, as a number or a range, and says when most of those who died were civilians. It names conquest, enslavement, forced conversion, expulsion and genocide where they happened. No side is glorified: a famous commander's card explains the cost of their victories, and the card never uses the winner's language of glory.

**Time spans.** Each row follows the rule in [spec.md section 5](spec.md#5-how-events-appear-in-the-world): "When" is the whole span the globe shows, and "Peak" is the phase of heaviest fighting or the decisive moment. A war that stopped and started again, such as the Punic Wars or the Hundred Years' War, lists its phases, each with its own dates and places; on the timeline the bar shows the pauses. Changes that outlast a war stay in the world after its end, such as new borders, ruins and walls.

**Genocides and crimes in wartime.** The Holocaust and the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki stay in [events.md](events.md) as events of their own, so that each has its own card written with special care; their war links to them. The same applies to the other genocides named in the rows below.

## People in wars

The column "People" links each war and battle to the people from [people.md](people.md) who took part in it or are remembered for it, with a few words on their part. The link is stored once, on the war or battle side, by the person's id from people.md. The data build fails when a name points nowhere, and builds the reverse links: a famous person's card lists the wars and battles they were part of, and double-clicking one flies there. A person is linked only if they took part, led, were directly caught up in the war or wrote a first-hand account of it, never just because they lived at the same time.

## Considered and left out

The Trojan War is legendary and is shown, if at all, as a story in the card of Troy, not as an event. The Cold War (1947–1991) is an era on the timeline ([eras.md](eras.md)), not a single war; its wars, such as Korea and Vietnam, are listed on their own. The Late Bronze Age collapse stays in [events.md](events.md) as a destruction event, because war was only one of its causes. Genocides that are not part of a war listed here, belong in the main event list as events of their own, like the Holocaust. The timeline ends on 26 December 1991 ([spec.md section 2.5](spec.md#25-range-and-start)), so wars after that are not shown: the Yugoslav Wars, which began in 1991 but were mostly fought later, the Second Congo War, the wars in Afghanistan from 2001 and Iraq from 2003, and wars still going on today. Many other wars, such as the Wars of the Roses, the Great Northern War, the Wars of the Spanish Succession, the Paraguayan War, the Nigerian Civil War and the Falklands War, are important but would crowd the list; they can be added later.
