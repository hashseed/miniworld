# The tree of humankind

This is the design of where ordinary people come from (spec section 2.4). It refines [spec.md](spec.md) and [milestone-2.md](milestone-2.md); where they disagree, fix whichever is wrong in the same pull request. Decisions marked **Proposed** are defaults chosen so work can start and are open for Yang to change.

Asked for by Yang on 2026-10-18: history starts with a single human at the cradle of humanity in Kenya, who hunts and builds temporary lodgings. As the population grows, more humans come out of these lodgings, and later out of houses, forts and palaces, so the globe holds one family tree of humankind. The tree is kept so that we can reason about what is going on.

Decided by Yang on 2026-10-18, on the first draft: the tree is computed by the data build and looked up by the app; the app opens at 70,000 BCE on the first person; Out of Africa moves to c. 60,000 BCE; Neanderthals are a second root; no population data is used to build the world, but the tree is checked against population estimates from time to time (section 6.1); a family tree view, a very detailed timeline of the tree, lets us skip to its key moments for debugging (section 6.2); foliage trees take a few years to grow and to disappear unless people fell them (section 7). Implementation waits until the one-clock spec and its implementation are merged.

## 1. The idea in one paragraph

At the very start of the timeline, 70,000 BCE, one person stands on the eastern shore of Lake Turkana in northern Kenya. She builds a grass hut, lights a fire and hunts. After a while a second person steps out of the hut, then a third. When the hut is full, two of them walk off and build a hut of their own a little further away, and people come out of that one too. Camp by camp the family spreads through Africa, then out of it, and on across the world. Every ordinary figure on the globe, from a mammoth hunter in Siberia to a factory worker in 1900, came out of some lodging and has a parent figure that was living there, so all of them form one tree that goes back to the first person. The tree is computed once, when the data is built, from the historical data; the app only looks it up, so the world stays a pure function of the clock.

## 2. Accuracy: what the first person stands for

Humankind never began with one person. Our species, _Homo sapiens_, arose in Africa about 300,000 years ago, and by 70,000 BCE people already lived across much of Africa. The first person is therefore a symbol, in the same way that one figure on the globe always stands for many real people (spec section 2.4): she stands for the first people of Africa. Her card says so plainly, and the opening of the tree is a **prologue** that tells a story of 200,000 years in about 8,000 (section 4.2). After the prologue the tree follows the historical data, as everything else in the world does.

- **Why Lake Turkana.** The Turkana basin, on the border of Kenya and Ethiopia, is one of the richest places on Earth for fossils of early humans and their relatives. The oldest well-dated _Homo sapiens_ bones of East Africa, Omo I, about 233,000 years old, come from the Omo valley just north of the lake (Vidal et al. 2022, _Nature_ 601). The name "Cradle of Humankind" is also used for a group of cave sites in South Africa; the card names Lake Turkana and says that early humans lived in many parts of Africa. The lake itself is too narrow for the simplified map and is not drawn (milestone 1 section 4.3).
- **Place.** Koobi Fora, on the eastern shore of the lake, at about 3.95° N, 36.20° E (**Proposed**).
- **Her look** (decided by Yang on 2026-10-18: a woman): the model `figure-first-person` of [milestone-2.md](milestone-2.md#133-the-prehistoric-model-set) section 13.3, an `african-stone-age` woman with a fixed look and a spear. Her red-ochre headband stays with her in every later era, whatever her clothes, so the root of the tree can always be found.
- **Founder's card** (draft, both languages):
  - en: "The first person in this world. Our species, _Homo sapiens_, began in Africa about 300,000 years ago, and some of its oldest bones were found near Lake Turkana. Real humankind did not start with one person: she stands for the first people of Africa. Everyone you will see on this globe comes from here."
  - de: "Der erste Mensch dieser Welt. Unsere Art, _Homo sapiens_, entstand vor etwa 300.000 Jahren in Afrika, und einige ihrer ältesten Knochen wurden nahe dem Turkana-See gefunden. In Wirklichkeit begann die Menschheit nicht mit einem einzigen Menschen: Sie steht für die ersten Menschen Afrikas. Alle, die du auf diesem Globus sehen wirst, stammen von hier."
- **Neanderthals: not shown for now** (Yang, 2026-10-20: "Do we even have neanderthals to begin with? If not, let's not add them"). The build has no Neanderthal tree, and none is planned; the paragraphs on them below stay as the design should they come later. Neanderthals are not descendants of the first person: their ancestors left Africa hundreds of thousands of years earlier. They are a second, smaller tree whose camps already stand in Europe and western Asia at the start of the timeline (section 3.5; decided by Yang on 2026-10-18). Their card says that most people living today carry a little Neanderthal ancestry, because the two met and had children; the tree, being a tree, does not draw these links.

## 3. What the tree holds

The tree is three tables, written by the data build (section 5) and read by the world query.

### 3.1 Figures

Every ordinary figure that ever appears is one row:

| Field    | Meaning                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `id`     | Whole number, in the order the figures appear (the first person is 0). Replaces the camp-and-index id of milestone 2 section 5.4                                                                                                                                   |
| `parent` | The figure it came from; none only for the roots                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| `born`   | Clock time at which it steps out of its lodging (a whole millisecond, so a whole day follows from it)                                                                                                                                                              |
| `home`   | Its lodgings over time: a list of (clock time, lodging id, spot); it walks from one to the next when the camp moves (section 4.4). The spot is its own place at the camp's hearth, the lowest one no other figure of the camp holds when it moves in (section 8.1) |
| `walks`  | Optional: the ways of its trips to an encounter and home again ([sites.md](sites.md#43-encounters) section 4.3), each as (clock time, lodging id, way): it walks to that home along the way instead of the lodging's own                                           |
| `branch` | The branch it belongs to (section 3.3), inherited from its parent unless it founds a new one                                                                                                                                                                       |
| `depth`  | Number of steps from its root: a measure of how far the family has come, not a count of real generations                                                                                                                                                           |
| `lost`   | Clock time and the event id if it is lost to war, famine, epidemic or disaster, or the day it dies before a burial (section 8.2); empty otherwise                                                                                                                  |
| `look`   | A hash seed for its colours and parts within its culture profile, as in milestone 2 section 5.2                                                                                                                                                                    |

A figure's culture, role and clothes at a date are not stored: they come from the culture area at its home and its profile at that date, with the per-figure offset inside the transition window that makes eras morph gradually (spec section 9). The tree only says who someone is, where they came from and where they live.

### 3.2 Lodgings

Every place people live is a lodging: a tent, a grass hut or lean-to at first, later houses, longhouses, farmsteads, town houses, forts and palaces.

| Field           | Meaning                                                                                                                                                                |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `id`            | Whole number                                                                                                                                                           |
| `camp`          | The camp or settlement it belongs to (lodgings around one hearth or inside one settlement)                                                                             |
| `at`            | Position in whole microdegrees (milestone 2 section 2.3)                                                                                                               |
| `built`, `gone` | Clock times at which it is put up and taken down or abandoned                                                                                                          |
| `builder`       | The figure who builds it; it walks there and the building beat plays (section 4.3)                                                                                     |
| `slot`          | Its place round the camp's hearth, the lowest one no other standing lodging of the camp holds, on land if one is free (section 8.1)                                    |
| `reach`         | For each of the five gaps between the camp's slots, how far out its people can go and stay on land, in layout metres up to 50, measured when it is built (section 8.1) |
| `kinds`         | Its model over time: a list of (clock time, kind), so a hut can be rebuilt as a house where the people stay                                                            |
| `capacity`      | How many figures it holds (section 4.1)                                                                                                                                |

The **kind** is chosen from the architecture set of the culture profile at the lodging's place and date, so the first person's hut is the approved `shelter-grass-hut` of `african-stone-age` and a Natufian family lives in a `shelter-natufian-house`. From the Neolithic on (milestone 3), the buildings of settlements are lodgings too: figures come out of town houses, the barracks of a fort, a palace or a monastery, according to the building's kind, and its people take the roles that fit it (soldiers from a fort, courtiers and servants from a palace). Landmarks without residents, such as a temple or a pyramid, are not lodgings.

### 3.3 Branches

A **branch** is a part of the tree with a name, which is what makes the tree something to reason about. A new branch starts when a figure founds a camp in a place its parent's branch has not reached, for one of these reasons:

- it is the first camp of a migration (milestone 2 section 9) beyond a named point of its path, such as the first camp in Arabia after the crossing of the Bab-el-Mandeb, the first in Sahul, the first in the Americas;
- it is the first camp of a new culture profile, for example where farming first takes over from hunting;
- it is the first lodging of a settlement record, such as the founding of Uruk or Rome.

Each branch row holds an id, its parent branch, the figure and clock time it starts with, its reason (a migration, culture or settlement id) and its localised name taken from that record. There are a few hundred branches over the whole of history (**Proposed** estimate), far fewer than figures.

### 3.4 What the tree is not

- It is not a population count, and no population data goes into it (decided by Yang on 2026-10-18). How many figures live where is set by the densities of the culture areas (milestone 2 section 3) and the size classes of settlements; the tree decides which figures these are, where they live and which figure each came from. Population estimates are used only to check the tree (section 6.1).
- It is not a family history of real people. Famous people are separate figures with their own records (spec section 6); they are not part of the tree.

### 3.5 Roots

| Root                                        | Figure 0 of the root                                    | Where and when                                                                                                |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Modern humans                               | The first person, `african-stone-age`                   | Koobi Fora at clock time 0 (1 January 70,000 BCE)                                                             |
| Neanderthals (not shown for now, section 2) | One figure per Neanderthal camp present at clock time 0 | The Neanderthal culture area at 70,000 BCE (milestone 2 section 4), with camps at full density from the start |

Should they come (they are not shown for now, section 2): Neanderthal camps grow no further after the start (decided by Yang on 2026-10-18). From about 45,000 BCE their branch thins out and its last camps disappear by about 38,000 BCE, as an event "Neanderthals disappear" whose card explains that the reasons are still debated (small and scattered groups, climate swings, competition with modern humans) and that their genes live on in us.

## 4. How the tree grows

Bands camp only at the precomputed sites of [sites.md](sites.md) and travel only along its links, and which sites they take, when they move and what they become is decided by its rules bound to region and time (Yang, 2026-10-19); where this section speaks of finding a spot, picking a free spot or moving away from water, sites.md section 4 says how it is done. The rules below run inside the data build (section 5), never in the app. They take as inputs the culture areas, densities and migrations of milestone 2 section 3 and 9, the land, sea, ice and desert at each date (milestone 1), settlements and their populations (later milestones), and the events that remove people.

### 4.1 Targets

For every place and date the data gives a **target**: how many camps a culture area should have and how many figures each should hold.

- **Camp spacing** follows the density of the culture area: **Proposed** about 600 km between camps for `medium`, 900 km for `low` 1,350 km for `sparse` (50% more than first proposed, Yang 2026-10-18: "make the villages stay 50% further from each other"), and 2,800 km for `remote`, the cold north of Asia, where far fewer people lived (Yang, 2026-10-19: "too many bands in siberia ... the population density is way lower there", then "let's only have 2-3"), measured on the true globe before the history lens. A culture area may set its own spacing (`spacingKm` in its record), overriding its density's: South America has 1,300 km, about 60% of the bands it had at `low` density (Yang, 2026-10-19: "way too many bands in south america. let's have only 60% as many"), and Australia with Tasmania 2,600 km, about three bands with those its routes place (sites.md section 4) ("too many in australia also ... 3 is sufficient"); New Guinea keeps `low`. With figures drawn at the size of trees (milestone 2 section 5.1), this keeps camps from overlapping at their drawn size; it replaces the camp cells of milestone 2 section 3.2. Beyond Africa, camps may only stand on land that the arrival map of [peopling.md](peopling.md#4-the-arrival-map) has opened and within the limits of their culture profile (decided by Yang on 2026-10-18).
- **Room on land.** A camp is drawn far larger than life, so a new camp or a band moving on picks only spots where the people's spots round the hearth (out to 7 layout metres, about 200 km, past the inner ring that a band of three to five never outgrows) are all on land, still on land when the band will move on (two minutes of watching later, as the sea moves), at least two of its five lodging slots have their lodging and builders on dry land (Yang, 2026-10-19: huts on the coast), and where the straight way from the old camp is land all the way, with no sea or lake on it (Yang, 2026-10-18: huts and people in the sea, people walking over water). Sea and lakes are water where the source grids or the globe's drawing of them from the finest terrain tiles say so, whichever reaches further, since the drawn shore can lie a sample further out (Yang, 2026-10-19: people gathering in Lake Malawi and Lake Tanganyika). Drawn rivers count as water for the camp itself: the hearth, the people's spots, the lodgings and the outings all keep off a river and a little of its bank (river field from 96 of 255; the globe draws the water from 128), checked on two rings round the hearth close enough that no river slips between their points (Yang, 2026-10-18: "people and villages still can end up in rivers"). A band on the move fords rivers, so a river does not stop people spreading. A camp fleeing the sea, ice or desert needs such a spot too, looking up to three spacings away, and moves away before the water reaches its people's spots; with no free spot it splits up among the nearest camps it can walk to, up to six spacings away, as many to each as fit on its inner ring of spots, in their lodgings with room or new ones beside them (peopling.md section 4). A band that stays where it is, settled at a Neolithic threshold or with nowhere free to move to, checks its land again at the end of each residence window and rebuilds its lodgings where the water has come closer to them, their outings or their people's spots. Lodgings take the lowest free slot whose place, builders, drying rack and the place behind the rack where hides are scraped are on dry land with dry ground to cross to it from the people's ring (a wedge of about 0.95 radians either side, checked every layout metre; else the lowest whose place and builders alone are dry), and each lodging records how far its people can go out on land through each gap between the slots (`reach`), measured on a ray every 0.05 radians across the gap and every layout metre out, so that no lake slips between the rays far out (Yang, 2026-10-19: people walked into Lake Malawi and Lake Tanganyika).
- **Figures per camp** are 3 to 5 at every density (milestone 2 section 3.3; decided by Yang on 2026-10-18), with a lodging holding up to 4 figures (**Proposed**) and a camp up to 5 lodgings, so a camp has one or two lodgings. **A village of farmers is bigger** (Yang, 2026-10-24: "we need to make sure the village has enough people to tend to the fields"): a camp of a Neolithic profile's culture grows to 6 to 10 figures (`VILLAGE_MIN` 6 plus a hash of its id below 5; 12 to 20 until Yang, 2026-10-25: "a village has way too many people in the neolithic. let's have half as many"), two or three lodgings, as the places and the usual growth allow; it buds off its small groups as before when it is full.
- **Settlements** (milestone 3 onwards) carry a size class over time in their records instead of population numbers: hamlet, village, town, city and great city, with **Proposed** targets of about 10, 20, 40, 80 and 150 figures, so a city of a million people has about ten times the figures of a village of a thousand, not a thousand times. The targets are tuned against the figure budget of spec section 9 when settlements arrive.

Rough world totals the targets give (**Proposed**, to check against the budget): 1 figure at 70,000 BCE, about 100 at the end of the prologue (Africa at `sparse` density, decided by Yang on 2026-10-18 so the globe is not crowded: about 25 camps of 3 to 5), about 500 at 50,000 BCE, about 1,000 at 10,000 BCE when nearly all habitable land is reached, and some tens of thousands by 1991. The view never holds more than a fraction of them.

### 4.2 The prologue

The first person is alone at first. Over the first minute of watching (about 550 years at regular speed; the tree build keeps her first companion inside her hut until a minute of watching has passed, whatever the curve of spec section 2.1 makes of it) she walks in from the lake shore, builds a grass hut, lights the hearth and goes hunting for an eland, and comes back to the fire (a new template `lone-hunt`, built from the approved `figure-african-stone-age-woman`, `shelter-grass-hut`, `prop-hearth` and `animal-eland` and the existing clips; **Proposed**). Then the second person steps out of the hut.

During the prologue the habitable land of Africa south of the Sahara band is reached by a **front** that spreads outward from Lake Turkana over land at a steady pace, so that the whole of it is inside the front by about 62,000 BCE (**Proposed**), about 15 minutes of watching at regular speed (spec section 2.1). Camps may be founded only behind the front. After 62,000 BCE the culture areas and migrations of milestone 2 alone decide where camps may be. The Out of Africa migration starts after the prologue, at c. 60,000 BCE (section 7).

### 4.3 Coming out of a lodging, and budding

The build steps through time in steps of 10 seconds of watching, converted to days by the curve of spec section 2.1 (so a step is decades in the deep past and days in the 20th century), and inside a step every action gets its exact clock time from a hash of stable inputs. Its rhythms are set in watching time, so camps look the same in every era (Yang, 2026-10-19: "the prehistoric people are busy just setting up camp and then packing up right away"); how fast people spread in years follows from them and the curve.

1. **Emergence.** A camp below its target lets one new figure come out of one of its lodgings that has room, at most one per lodging every minute of watching (**Proposed**). Its parent is a figure living in that lodging, chosen by hash. In the world the new figure steps out of the doorway and joins the camp's work; nobody pops into view (spec section 5).
2. **A new lodging.** When every lodging of a camp is full and the camp has fewer lodgings than its band size allows, a figure of the camp builds another lodging beside the hearth.
3. **Budding.** At most every three minutes of watching, when a camp is at its full size and there is free habitable land within reach, two or three of its figures (chosen by hash, never the root, and always leaving at least one behind) walk off together and found a new camp (decided by Yang on 2026-10-18: small groups, to avoid crowding) at the best free spot at least one camp spacing away and at most two spacings away (**Proposed**). Spots are scored as in milestone 2 section 3.2: near a river, lake or coast is better, steep or high ground worse, and open ground better than woodland (the tree density of the foliage slice, foliage.md section 2; Yang, 2026-10-19: "they prefer to set up camp where there are no trees"). The first of them to arrive is the builder. If the new camp starts a branch (section 3.3), the branch is recorded.
4. **No growth beyond the target.** A camp at its target only works and moves; it never sheds figures to reach a lower target.

### 4.4 Moving, and losing people

- **Camps move.** Before villages, a camp moves to a new spot inside its area at the end of every residence window, one to two minutes of watching, chosen by hash (Yang, 2026-10-19: "I want them to move every 1-2 watching minute, and have other activities in between"): these are nomads, and a band packing up and walking on should be seen often. Its figures take down their lodgings, walk together to the new spot and build them again. The tree records the move as new home entries and new lodgings, so a band is seen walking, never fading out and back in.
- **The land changes.** When the sea rises over a camp, ice advances over it or the land turns to desert, the camp moves to the nearest habitable spot before that happens; nobody is lost to it. The Green Sahara (spec section 3) opens land to camps between about 9000 and 3500 BCE, and as it dries the camps move away, south or to the Nile.
- **Lost figures.** People never die of age (spec section 2.4). War, famine, epidemic and disaster events remove the share of figures in their area that the record gives; which figures are lost is decided by hash, so it is the same every time (spec section 9). A lost figure sinks down and fades out, with no blood (spec section 4). The first person is never lost (**Proposed**), so the root of the tree can always be found, still living by Lake Turkana and taking on each new era's role there.
- **Refilling.** After a loss the camp is below its target again, and new figures come out of its lodgings as the population recovers, at the pace the data gives.

## 5. The tree build

The tree is computed by the data build (`npm run build-data`), in a step after the culture areas, migrations, settlements and events are validated.

Until the culture profile records of milestone 2 exist, the build reads the areas it needs from `data/curated/culture-areas.json`: per culture, its density and one polygon per snapshot date, each with a source. The first entry is `african-stone-age` at `sparse` density in Africa without Madagascar (south of the Sahara band until the prologue front opens the rest); the others are the culture areas of [peopling.md](peopling.md#51-culture-profiles-are-the-adaptations) section 5.1, each also naming its profile's limits (section 5.2 there: the coldest annual mean temperature, the highest ground) and its arrival points. The habitat (land at the date, no ice, aridity under 0.8, below the area's highest ground, 3,500 m unless it says otherwise, and no colder than its coldest temperature, **Proposed**) cuts each down to where people could live, and the arrival map of peopling.md section 4 to where they had arrived. The build writes the whole tree to `data/build/tree/tree.json` (git-ignored) for the commands of section 6, alongside the chunks the app loads.

- **Deterministic.** The build uses only whole-number arithmetic, the hash of `app/src/core/hash.ts` and the deterministic maths of `app/src/core/detmath.ts` (milestone 2 section 2.3), never `Math.random` or the wall clock. The same inputs give a byte-identical tree on every machine, and a test stores the hash of the canonical serialisation.
- **Output.** Figures and lodgings are split into chunks by place and time window like all other content (spec section 10): a figure belongs to every chunk whose tile it lives in during that time window. Branches are small and go into a single chunk loaded with the first figures. Rough size (**Proposed** estimate): under 1 MB compressed for the whole of history.
- **Checks.** The build fails when any of these is false:
  - every figure but a root has a parent that is alive and living in the lodging the figure comes out of, at the moment it comes out;
  - every lodging stands on land that is habitable at its date for its whole life, and no two camps are closer than the spacing allows;
  - no figure lives outside a culture area that allows people there at the date, and no branch reaches a continent before its migration does (milestone 2 section 3.1);
  - each camp's figure count stays within its target, apart from losses still recovering;
  - no two standing lodgings of a camp share a slot and no two figures living in a camp share a spot (section 8.1);
  - the tree has exactly two roots, and every figure reaches one of them through its parents.

## 6. Keeping track: reasoning about the tree

The tree is meant to be looked at, both by us while building the world and in a simple form by the viewer.

- **Report.** `npm run tree -- report` writes a readable summary of the built tree to `data/build/tree/report.md` (git-ignored): figures, camps, births, moves and losses per world region and time window; every branch with its date, place, first figure and parent branch; losses per event; and the results of the checks of section 5.

### 6.1 Checking against population estimates

No population data is used to build the tree, but we check from time to time that it tells the right story (Yang, 2026-10-18). `npm run tree -- check-population` compares the tree with a small curated table of published population estimates per world region and date (`data/curated/population-check.yaml`, each row with its source, for example world totals of about a few hundred thousand to a million people at 70,000 BCE, about 5 million at 10,000 BCE, about 200 million at 1 CE and 5.4 billion in 1991). Since one figure stands for more people the more people there are, the check does not expect the figure count to match; it expects the share of the world's figures in each region to follow the share of the world's people there, damped the same way as the settlement targets (**Proposed**: the cube root), and the growth to happen in the same periods. It lists every region and date where the tree is off by more than a factor of 2 (**Proposed**). It is a report for us, not a build failure, and runs whenever the culture areas, densities or settlements change a lot.

### 6.2 The family tree view

Once the world is fully populated the tree holds tens of thousands of figures, so it is shown as a very detailed timeline that lets us jump straight to its key moments for debugging (Yang, 2026-10-18).

- **Where.** In development builds the address flag `?tree` opens a panel above the timeline, with the same axis as the timeline (spec section 7.1), zoomable down to single years.
- **Lanes.** Each branch is a lane, nested under its parent branch and drawn from its first figure to the end of the timeline, with the number of its figures over time as the lane's thickness. Lanes can be folded and filtered by world region.
- **Key moments** are marks on the lanes, generated by the tree build: a branch starting (a migration passing a named point, a new culture, a settlement founded), a Neolithic threshold reached ([peopling.md](peopling.md#36-neolithic-thresholds) section 3.6), the first lodging of each kind in a region (the first hut in Asia, the first house, fort or palace), losses to an event, a camp moving away from rising sea, ice or desert, and failed or near-failed checks of section 5.
- **Jumping.** Clicking a mark moves the clock to that moment and flies the camera to its place; clicking a lane shows its line back to the root, as `lineage` does. Selecting a figure on the globe highlights its lane.
- **Data.** The key moments are a small list per time window, in the tree's chunks (section 5); the view loads them as it scrolls, like the rest of the content.

A view of the tree for viewers, such as a "Family tree of humankind" button, may follow later; for now the tree is for development and the family line on the figure card.

- **Questions.** `npm run tree -- at <date> [--region <id>]` lists the camps and figures alive then, and `npm run tree -- lineage <figure id>` prints the figure's line back to its root, shortened to the branch points ("figure 1,822, Danube 40,000 BCE ← first camp in Europe 45,100 BCE ← crossing of the Bab-el-Mandeb 60,000 BCE ← Lake Turkana 70,000 BCE").
- **Tree overlay for development.** In development builds, the address flag `?tree` draws lines on the globe from each camp to the camp it budded from, coloured by branch, for the camps founded in the time window in view, so the spread of humankind can be watched and checked.
- **On the figure card.** The card of every ordinary figure adds one line about its family, in both languages, built from its branch chain, for example "Her family's line goes back to the first person at Lake Turkana and left Africa about 60,000 BCE." / "Die Linie ihrer Familie reicht zurück bis zum ersten Menschen am Turkana-See und verließ Afrika um 60.000 v. Chr." Double-tapping the line flies to the camp where the branch began, at its founding date (**Proposed**).

## 7. Changes to the rest of the spec

- **Start of the timeline** (spec section 2.5): the timeline already begins at 70,000 BCE; the app now opens there, at clock time 0, at Lake Turkana, close enough to see the first person, instead of at 50,000 BCE (decided by Yang on 2026-10-18). The first load stays within the 3 MB of spec section 10, since the opening view needs the African tiles, one camp and its models.
- **Out of Africa** moves from c. 70,000 BCE to c. 60,000 BCE ([events.md](events.md) and milestone 2 section 12), close to the mainstream genetic estimate of a main dispersal between about 60,000 and 50,000 years ago; the card mentions earlier groups whose lines died out. **The first people in Sahul** are shown at c. 50,000 BCE, with older dates such as Madjedbebe (about 65,000 years ago) named as debated.
- **Milestone 2** builds the tree for modern humans and Neanderthals up to 9000 BCE in place of its camp cells, residence fades and figure ids (milestone 2 sections 3, 5.4 and 15), together with the report, the population check and the family tree view of section 6. No population data is used (decided by Yang on 2026-10-18).
- **Foliage** ([foliage.md](foliage.md#4-placement) section 4): a tree takes a few years to grow and a few years to disappear (**Proposed** 5 years each, about 12 seconds at regular speed), unless people fell it, which takes a moment: where a camp, lodging or field is founded on a tree's spot, the builders fell it and it tips over and is gone within half a year of the timeline (decided by Yang on 2026-10-18).
- **Milestone 3 onwards** add lodgings of farming villages and settlements, and figures coming out of houses, forts and palaces.

## 8. Performance

Nothing about the tree is simulated in the app. The world query loads the tree chunks for the tiles and time window in view and keeps the figures whose `born` is before the clock time and whose `lost` is after it, which is a lookup over a short sorted list per chunk. The figure limits and levels of detail of spec section 9 and milestone 2 section 5.5 are unchanged. Tree chunks are world data like culture areas: the query shows a tile's ordinary figures once its chunk has arrived and never guesses them, so loading changes when figures are drawn, never who they are.

The tree's chunks are split by time only: one file per minute of watching (**Proposed**; one per window of the timeline axis while the tree held only Africa), each holding the lodgings standing and the figures alive in it and in the minute before (for walks under way), with each figure's homes up to the first after the window, since what it does before a move depends on the move (Yang, 2026-10-20: "occasionally I see people's animations stop and replaced by a completely new animation, which resets the position of the people very abruptly": until then the scene in a window's last moments differed between its file and the next, and people jumped when the app moved on), and the app loads the file of the clock's minute and the next. Once the tree spreads beyond Africa (peopling.md), the app works out only the camps near the view each frame. The camp scene (who stands where, doing what, `treeScene`) is worked out in a Web Worker (Yang, 2026-10-20: optimizations, without shared memory): the app sends each tree file to the worker once, asks it each frame for the scene at the clock's time on the camera's side of the globe, and draws the newest answer while the clock runs, at most a few frames old. While the clock stands still, after a jump, or where no answer for the time is at hand, the app works the scene out itself, so a paused picture is always exact and the same as the worker's. The split by tile follows if a minute's file grows past about 100 KB compressed; the data build fails when one does, so the split is made when it starts to pay. Measured on the real build of 2026-10-25, before the split was due: a minute holds at most about 170 camps, 620 lodgings and 565 figures, 26 KB Brotli (median 24 KB), so a whole minute is less than one level-2 terrain tile's share of the first view. Split by level-1 tiles it would be 15 files of at most 31 camps and 5.5 KB each, before the camps each tile must carry from its neighbours (the campmates and destinations of its walkers, the camps near its border that `nearOtherCamp` and the visits look at, the scenes at anchors its people join), with about 45 figures a minute walking between tiles. Yang asked for "smooth streaming without visual jank" (2026-10-25): one file per minute arrives whole, two requests a minute instead of ten to twenty, and no figures can show up tile by tile, so the tree stays split by time until the limit is reached. When it is, the split is by tile as in spec section 10, each tile's file carries everything the scene of its own camps reads, a figure walking between tiles is in both files, the app works out each tile's scene on its own and merges them by id, and `world/sceneParity.test.ts` and the golden tests check that a figure's pose is the same from either file and that the merged scene equals the whole minute's.

**Where the scene's code lives.** `treeScene(chunk, ms, startDay, near)` in `app/src/world/treeScene.ts` composes the scene: it works out the lookups of one call (the chunk's memo, who lives where, each figure's stays and turns) and asks the parts for poses. The parts live in `app/src/world/scene/`, one module per part of the camp's life:

| Module | Holds |
| --- | --- |
| `types.ts` | the chunk the scene reads and the poses it returns: the contract with the renderer |
| `units.ts` | scales, paces, places and looks |
| `camp.ts` | the ring of lodgings and spots, timings of the camp's work, outings' lanes, held tools, the camp events' places |
| `memo.ts` | what is worked out once per chunk: stays, places, villages |
| `stays.ts` | a figure's stays at camps and its walks between them |
| `turns.ts` | what a figure goes off to do in each turn of its day |
| `poses.ts` | where a figure is and what it does at a clock time |
| `campEvents.ts` | catch, dances, visits and feasts |
| `hunt.ts`, `huntPlans.ts` | prey, throws and bow shots; who hunts when, and the hunt's plan and props |
| `stages.ts`, `stageEvents.ts` | the scenes at anchors of section 8.2 and their sessions |
| `villageLife.ts` | the fields' jobs of a Neolithic village and what the village shows |
| `routes.ts`, `outings.ts` | walks round obstacles; which outing a figure takes |
| `context.ts` | `SceneCx`, the lookups of one call that the parts share |

A part is a plain function that takes the members of `SceneCx` it needs (`Pick<SceneCx, …>`) and its own arguments; `treeScene()` builds the `SceneCx` once per call and keeps a short wrapper of each part under its old name, so the parts call each other as before and a scene is the same whatever module a part lives in. A new kind of activity or scene is a new part, or an addition to the module it belongs to, not more code in `treeScene.ts`. These modules are world code: the lint rules of milestone-2 section 2.3 apply to them as to the rest of `app/src/world/`.

### 8.1 Drawing a camp

A camp is drawn on a stage 1.7 times larger than the old vignette stages (milestone 2 section 5.1): figures about 72 km per metre (42.5 there; 85 and 50 until Yang asked on 2026-10-19 to make people, camps, trees and animals 15% smaller), the layout 3 times smaller, so people, lodgings and hearths are 70% larger than on a stage and the whole camp grows with them (Yang, 2026-10-18: "make the humans and their buildings/fire places 70% larger"). The hearth stands at the camp's point; each lodging stands on a ring around it (12 layout metres, close round the fire but clear of the people; Yang 2026-10-18: "put the huts and fireplace a bit closer together", and 16 until Yang 2026-10-19: "make the camps a bit more compact by moving huts closer to the fire") in one of five evenly spaced places, its `slot` (every lodging model is drawn at most 2.7 m across, so the larger shelters of other cultures, such as the hide tent, fit the same layout), turned by the hash of the camp, with its door towards the hearth; each figure stands at its own spot by the hearth, one of 8 on an inner ring (5 layout metres, filled opposite sides first so a small band spreads round the fire) and then 12 on an outer ring (8.5 layout metres), spaced so that figures do not cover each other, and changes every 7 to 13 seconds of watching between standing (`idle`, a quarter of the time), sitting by the fire (`sit`, a quarter), tending it (`work`, a fifth), cracking nuts on a stone (`crack`, a tenth), telling a story with their hands (`point`, facing the fire, a twelfth) and, for grown-ups and the first person, knapping a stone hand axe (`knap`, an eighth: kneeling, a flint hand axe on the knee struck with a hammerstone; Yang, 2026-10-18: "can they also build tools like stone hand ax?"; a third of the time until Yang, 2026-10-21: "people spend too much time on stone tools by the fire. they should do more diverse things"; children sit instead), easing from one clip to the next (milestone 2 section 5.3). No two lodgings and no two standing figures ever overlap (Yang, 2026-10-18): the build hands out slots and spots so that none is shared, and checks it (section 5). Figures act three times faster than life (`TEMPO`, Yang 2026-10-18: "speed up their actions by 3x"): clips play three times as fast and figures cover ground three times as fast, so their feet still do not slide, and the times below are already sped up. All of it is a pure function of the clock time:

- **Coming out.** A new figure appears inside its lodging when it is born, crouches out through the door and walks to its spot.
- **Walking.** When a figure's home moves to a lodging somewhere else (the camp moves, or a group buds off), it walks there from the moment of the move, at the pace its stride gives at its drawn size so its feet do not slide, about 2 to 3 seconds of watching for a move of a few hundred kilometres. A new lodging appears when its builder arrives, and an old one is taken down by the figures leaving it last (see **Taking down**). On the way, each figure keeps its place in the group: it sets off from where it stood and arrives where it will work.
- **Pace.** Figures act and walk 3.6 times faster than life (`TEMPO`; 3 until Yang asked on 2026-10-19 for them to move about 20% faster), their clips played faster to match, so feet still do not slide; every duration below in seconds of watching already includes it.
- **A new camp.** The trees of a new place have thinned out before the band arrives (sites.md section 6; until Yang, 2026-10-21, the band felled them on arrival, everyone chopping for about 4 seconds while the trees tipped over). The first builder kneels at the hearth and lights the fire with flint stones (the `strike` clip) for about 2.5 seconds, sparks flying from the stones, and the flames rise from nothing as it catches (Yang, 2026-10-19: "they use flint stones to light camp fires", at new camps only). Only then does the first hut go up; the others start building as soon as they arrive.
- **Building.** The figures that move into a new lodging on the day it is built build it together: when the builder arrives, they gather round it on the hearth's side, clear of its walls and evenly spread so they do not overlap, face it and work (`work` clip) while it goes up over about 2.7 seconds of watching (about a year): a lodging model with building steps (bones `build-0`, `build-1`, ... in milestone 2 section 13.3) shows them in order, each growing up from its base in an equal share of the time, so the brush hut gets its frame of bent branches first and then its grass from the ground up; a model without steps rises whole. Then the builders walk to their own spots (decided by Yang on 2026-10-18: the band builds its huts, frame and walls first, then the roof).
- **Taking down.** Before a band moves on, the figures that leave a lodging last take it down: over the last 1.7 seconds of watching before they leave (about eight months), they stand round it as they did to build it and work while its building steps go away in reverse order, so the hut is gone, not faded, when they set off (Yang, 2026-10-18: continuity when a camp moves).
- **Camp life.** Between building and taking down, each figure's time is cut into turns of about 17 seconds of watching, shifted by its hash, and each turn is a rest at its spot (idle, work or knap, as above) or an activity chosen by hash (Yang, 2026-10-19: the activities below), taken only if it fits before the figure must be busy again and only as far as its gap has land (`reach`), so nobody walks into the sea or a lake; one that does not fit falls back to a shorter one or a rest:
  - **Gathering** (women and children): out through its gap to about 22 layout metres, pick for about 2.5 seconds, back carrying the load.
  - **Hunting** (men on about one turn in four, the first person on about one in six; two in five and a quarter until Yang, 2026-10-20: "activities are often just hunting. what about other activities?"): see **Hunts** below.
  - **Firewood** (men and women): out to about 20 metres among the trees, break branches (`work`) for about 2 seconds, back carrying the bundle.
  - **Fetching water** (women): only where its camp has water within reach, out through the gap whose land ends nearest, to its last metre of land, kneel to fill (`work`) for about 1.5 seconds, back carrying.
  - **Scraping a hide** (women): at the drying rack behind its own lodging, where it has one (the approved `prop-drying-rack`, standing about 18 layout metres from the hearth while its lodging stands, out of every lane; behind about one lodging place in three, chosen by the hash of the camp and place, since Yang said on 2026-10-19: "every camp has hides hung up. that's weird. let's only have some"; women of a lodging without one do not scrape; Yang, 2026-10-19: a kill was carried through a hide hung out to dry), walk round the end of the rack to stand behind its middle and scrape the hide (the `scrape` clip) for about 4 seconds, then back round the end.
  - **Cracking nuts** (women and children): sit by the hearth at its spot and crack nuts between two stones (the `crack` clip) for about 4 seconds.
  - **Making a spear** (men): at its spot, knap a stone point (`knap`) for about 3 seconds, then bind it to the shaft (`work`) for about 2.
  - **Cooking** (grown-ups): only while the kill of a hunt lies at its camp; the hunter who brought a kill home on the carrying pole always roasts it in its next turn, unless an earlier kill of the camp still lies there and holds the spit (Yang, 2026-10-20: "after a hunt where the carcass is carried to the fireplace, can we have a spitroast if the fireplace is not yet taken?"): walk to it, butcher (`work`) for about 2 seconds, carry the meat to the hearth and roast it on a spit over the fire (`work`, the new prop `prop-spit` above the fire) for about 4 seconds, then back to its spot.
  - **Fishing** (men on about one turn in twelve, women on one in twenty-five; Yang, 2026-10-20: "can we have coastal people fish in the ocean or lake?"): only where the camp has water within reach, as for fetching water: out to the water's edge, stand watching with the spear raised for about 1.5 seconds, stab down (`work`) for about 0.7, twice, then back carrying the catch.
  - **Skinning a kill** (grown-ups, where their lodging has a rack): only while the kill of a hunt lies at its camp: walk to it, skin it (`work`) for about 2.5 seconds, carry the hide to the drying rack behind its lodging and hang it (`work`) for about 1.3 seconds, then back to its spot.
  - **Taking a hide down** (men and women, where their lodging has a rack): at the rack behind its lodging, take a dried hide down (`work`) for about 1.3 seconds, carry it to the door, crouch in and stay inside for about 2 seconds, then crouch out and walk back.
  - **Into the hut** (everyone, on about one turn in ten, a child's in seven): walk to its lodging's door, outside the walls on the hearth side, crouch through it (the `crouch` clip) and stay inside, out of sight, for about 4 seconds, then crouch out and walk back.
  - **A stroll** (everyone): to about 13 metres and back.
- **Catch** (Yang, 2026-10-19: "children play with each other"). Never at a camp on a steep mountainside, where the ground rises or falls more than 600 m from the hearth out to the ring of catch (Yang, 2026-10-20: "I have kids running up the himalayas for playing catch"); the build marks such lodgings `steep`. A camp event on the camp's own rhythm of about 17 seconds, shifted by its hash: in about one window in three, when two or more of its children are free for the whole window, they play catch for about 10 seconds in the gap with the most land, on a ring about 7 layout metres from the hearth, between the people's places and the lodgings (10.5 until Yang asked on 2026-10-19 for smaller rings), or on the dancers' ring where the land round the camp is short: all run round the ring (`run`), and in turn the one who is "it" cuts across to catch the next, about every 2.5 seconds, and rejoins the ring. Their own activities that would overlap it are dropped.
- **Dancing** (Yang, 2026-10-19). A camp event on the same rhythm, in about one window in six (one in twelve until 2026-10-20; and never with catch): everyone free for the whole window dances in a ring about 4.5 layout metres round the fire (the `dance` clip; 6.5 until Yang asked on 2026-10-19 for smaller rings), turning slowly round it, for about 10 seconds.
- **Hunts** (Yang, 2026-10-19: spears; two people carry the kill home on a pole; bows from about 45,000 BCE, see the bow paragraph below). A hunt is a camp event: the hunter and a helper, one other grown-up of the same camp who is free for the whole hunt and not hunting then, chosen by hash (lower hunter ids choose first); the helper's own outings that would overlap it are dropped. A zebra or an eland, chosen by hash, comes out of the trees at the far end of the hunter's lane about 8 seconds of watching before the hunters set out and walks in to graze side on about 34 layout metres out (Yang, 2026-10-20: "they should spawn and roam for some time before being hunted"); no hunt is held whose animal would come out or graze within 25 layout metres of another camp standing then, where its people are ("they often spawn on top of people"); close enough to be seen with the camp (Yang, 2026-10-18: the hunted animals were missing; "hunters should hunt african animals like zebras, elands, etc."). Both walk out, the helper a few metres behind; the hunter stops about 8 metres from the animal and throws his spear (the `throw` clip), and the spear flies its whole way in an arc from the hand to the animal, as in the old vignettes. About one throw in three misses, chosen by hash: the spear sticks in the ground a few metres beyond the animal, which bolts about 6 metres further out and stands again; the hunter walks to the spear, pulls it out, walks closer and throws again, and that throw hits. A hit animal falls where it stands (its `fall` clip) with the spear in it. Hunter and helper kneel on either side of it and bind its legs to a carrying pole (about 3 metres): over a few seconds its legs fold together, the hunter pulls his spear out and the pole is laid along the bound legs. Then they stand and lift the pole onto their shoulders, the animal hanging upside down beneath it (its `hung` pose), and carry it back in step, one at each end, at a carrying pace, to a place about 12 metres from the hearth in the hunter's gap, between the people and the lodgings, where they lay it down and walk back to their spots; it fades away there after a while. Without a helper free, the hunter butchers the kill alone where it fell and walks back carrying the meat, as before. The hunt keeps to the hunter's lane, so it needs land to about 44 metres there as before. The pole is a new prop, `prop-carry-pole`, which needs Yang's approval like every model.
  - **Hunted animals stand on the highest ground under them** (issue 172: a fallen mammoth was sunk so that only its tusks showed). A hunted animal is many kilometres long on the drawn globe, so the ground at its middle alone would bury an end on a slope: its height is the highest ground at its middle and at four points round it, 7 layout metres out for a mammoth and 3 for other prey.
  - **Bows** (Yang, 2026-10-22, issue 142: "In the second half of the paleolithic, also use bows, starting from around 45000 BCE. Bows animation also need to have arrows. Larger animals like deers or zebras need two arrows to kill. First arrow makes them move a bit but become slower."): from 45,000 BCE hunters of every culture except the Neanderthals' (who keep their thrusting spears) hunt with a bow instead of throwing a spear; the hunt is otherwise as above. Instead of the `throw` clip the hunter plays `shoot` (about 2.4 seconds): he raises the bow in his left hand, draws an arrow back to the cheek with his right and looses it at about 1.1 seconds. The bow and the arrow on it are drawn in his hands for the gesture; the arrow then flies the whole way from the string to the animal in a low arc, like a spear, and sticks in it or in the ground. A missed arrow is left in the ground (the hunter does not stop to pull it out). A zebra, an eland, a reindeer or an aurochs, the larger game, takes two arrows (a gazelle, a kangaroo or a guanaco takes one): the first hits and the animal does not fall; it starts away at a slow walk, about 4 seconds, to the far spot (the same spot a missed animal bolts to), and stands there; the hunter walks after it to his second place and shoots again, and that arrow kills it. The first arrow stays in the animal as it walks. A miss, if the hash gives one, comes first as before, so a large animal can take a missed and two hitting arrows. The mammoth party shoots too, one arrow each. A bow is not a spear: the hunter's own spear stays in his hand-side `tool` bone, hidden during `shoot`, and appears again after it. The bow and arrow are drawn as their own small models (`prop-bow`, `prop-arrow`) and need Yang's approval like every model.
  - **The rest of the herd** (Yang, 2026-10-19: "when a group of animal is hunted and the surviving animals scatter, they should not fade right away but stay around for a bit longer before fading individually"). One or two of the hunted animal's herd walk in beside it and graze. When the first spear lands they bolt, each to its own spot aside, a little short of the treeline where they came from, chosen by hash. There each stands looking back towards the camp for about 3 to 5½ seconds, its own time by hash, and then fades on its own.
  - **Aurochs** (Yang, 2026-10-20: "humans also hunt aurochs"): the hunters of the Near East (with the Natufians) and the ice-age hunters of Europe hunt aurochs, the wild cattle, as well as gazelles or reindeer, chosen by hash, with the approved `animal-aurochs`.
  - **A mammoth takes a party** (Yang, 2026-10-20: "mammoths need multiple hunters and multiple spears to go down"): the hunter and two or three more grown-ups of the camp who are free for the whole hunt stand in a row across the lane, about 4.5 layout metres apart, and throw one after another, about 0.6 seconds apart; every spear sticks in the mammoth, which rears and stamps (its `struggle` clip) from the first and goes down with the last. They butcher it together round it and carry the meat home. Without at least two more free, the hunter goes after other game.
  - **Mammoth hunts** (Yang, 2026-10-19: "implement mammoth hunting"). The ice-age hunters of Europe and the hunters of Siberia and Beringia hunt mammoths as well as reindeer, chosen by hash, while mammoths lived on the mainland, until 10,000 BCE (wildlife.md section 2), and only in a lane with land out to its full 50 layout metres. A mammoth is too big for the pole. It grazes further out (38 metres, bolting to 42), the hunter throws from about 7 life-size metres, and the herd keeps further apart. Hunter and helper kneel in front of and behind the kill along the lane (to either side they could stand off the lane's land), butcher it where it fell for about 2 seconds, and each carries meat home. Its remains lie there for about 5½ seconds more before they fade. Hunting the mammoth is well attested, for example at Lugovskoye in West Siberia, where a mammoth vertebra holds a stone point (Zenin et al. 2006).
- **Round the fire, the lodgings and the racks.** No walk passes through anything (Yang, 2026-10-19: "make sure clipping is correct"): a path that would come within 3 layout metres of the middle of the hearth, 4.6 of a lodging's or 4.4 of a drying rack's bends round it by a point beside it, and each part of the bent path is checked again; round a lodging place or a rack it never bends away from the hearth but goes by the fire's side, since the land behind an empty lodging place is not checked and may be sea (a walk in the Negev went round an empty place 350 km out over the Mediterranean, 2026-10-20); only what the walk starts or ends at (a door, a rack) is not avoided. A carrying pole keeps to its lane, the middle of a gap between lodgings, so its two carriers stay in step. The test "nothing passes through anything" checks that walkers, carried kills and poles keep clear of lodgings, racks and fires. A band moving on first walks round its fire to the edge of the camp facing the new place (7 layout metres out, inside the ring of lodgings), crosses the land from there as a loose group, not on one line (Yang, 2026-10-20: "multiple people bunched together"): by its spot at the new camp, each walker leaves and comes in 2.2 layout metres to one side of the camps' gate points, in the middle or to the other side, keeps that side along a route's way, closing in on the open way to within 0.45 layout metres of the line, the strip a link is checked for water (2026-10-21: a walker beside a way along Lake Nicaragua's shore was in the lake), and walks in rows of three 3 metres apart (never further behind than a twentieth of the walk), the rows forming over the first tenth of the way and closing up over the last, and comes in at the edge of the new camp facing the old one before it walks round the new fire to its work, so nobody walks over a fire on the way out or in (Yang, 2026-10-18).
- **The fire.** (Only while a hunter-gatherer lodging of the place stands; a Neolithic village has none, section 8.5.) The hearth's flames flicker, each at its own pace, with the clock, so they hold still while the world is paused.
- **Trees.** A camp keeps the trees clear for about 510 drawn kilometres around its hearth (the ring of lodgings and a margin) while it stands. Over the 10 seconds of watching before a band arrives at a place, the trees there wither and shrink away (sites.md section 6); when it leaves, they grow back over about five years (12 seconds), so the place a camp left is not instantly forest again (section 7 and [foliage.md](foliage.md) section 4).
- **Lighter ground.** From orbit the ground of a camp is lighter, so the huts and people on it read as shapes and not as a dark smudge (Yang, issue 177: "fully zoomed out, the villages and the humans look like smudges"; "with zoom, we color the ground below each village lighter"). A soft, warm patch lies on the terrain under every camp that stands, about 0.6 of the camp's clearing (300 drawn kilometres) in radius, lightest at the hearth and fading to nothing at its rim, with no outline. It follows the drawn ground, is left out where the ground is under water, and grows with the camera's altitude: nothing below 1,500 km, full strength from 4,500 km. It is a drawing aid only: it changes no ground height, takes no part in any rule, and is the same for every culture.
- **Upright.** Figures stand upright on the ground whatever the camera does, as on stages (milestone 2 section 5.1), and keep their size at every zoom.

### 8.2 Scenes at anchors

**Nobody is ever teleported** (Yang, 2026-10-21): in every scene, whoever has a place to be walks there from where they actually are, leaving early enough to arrive in time (one who cannot goes unused), and walks back; a walk always begins with a rest at the walker's own spot until their departure, never with a position they were not at.

**No rebuild in session** (2026-10-25): an anchor's camp that must rebuild its lodgings in place, the sea having reached them or their lanes, waits while one of its scene's sessions is under way (from the session's start for a whole turn, with the time to take down and build round it) and rebuilds after it, so the people the session needs are not taking their huts down instead (Lascaux had no painter for a session when a rebuild fell in it). A band whose site floods still leaves at once.

Yang asked on 2026-10-20 for cave painting and burials ("do we have cave painting yet?"; "implement everything": the scenes at Leang Karampuang, Chauvet and Sungir). Before there are villages, the stories of the deep past are told by the camps themselves: an anchor (sites.md section 4.1) may carry a **scene**, which the band held at its site plays as part of its camp life. A scene is not a vignette: it has no cast of its own, only the anchor's band, so nobody is drawn twice and the band's people are the ones who paint or bury.

| Field of `scene` | Meaning                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `kind`           | `cave-painting`, `burial`, `monument` or `bone-hut`                                                                                                                                                                               |
| `prop`           | The model it shows: for a cave painting its wall, `prop-cave-wall-sulawesi`, `prop-cave-wall-chauvet` or `prop-cave-wall-lascaux`; for a burial `prop-burial`, for a monument `prop-gobekli-ring`, for a bone hut `prop-bone-hut` |

- **Its place at the camp.** The scene stands behind one of the camp's lodging places, out of every lane, on the side of the camp towards the real cave or grave (the anchor's place): the wall or grave 12 layout metres beyond the ring of lodgings, facing the hearth, so 24 metres out at a full camp, 21 at a compact one and 18.2 at a minimal one; where the land is too narrow for that, 10 or 8 metres beyond it. At a compact camp the scene is three quarters of its size, all its places and distances beyond the ring with it, and at a minimal camp half, as their lodgings are smaller (Leang Karampuang's camp is a minimal one). Of the lodging places with no drying rack where the prop, the people's places in front of it and the way there from beside the lodging (out of the gap at 4 metres past the ring) stay dry land for the anchor's whole stay, the build takes the one pointing most nearly towards the anchor's place (Yang, 2026-10-20: the Chauvet cave stood north-east of its fire, while the real cave lies south of the camp's site); without one the build fails, naming the anchor. An anchor may name a separate place for its camp (`camp`, a `lat` and `lon`), where its site is laid and chosen instead of at the anchor's place, so the camp stands where it should beside the cave: Chauvet's about 60 km south-east of the cave, near the Rhône, the place nearby where its cut keeps clear of water (Yang, 2026-10-21: "move the cave away from the water"; west of the Ardèche gorge until then, Yang 2026-10-20: "place the fireplace a bit more to the west"). The cave stands for the real one: at a drawn metre to about 24 km, the camp covers the land round the cave, and on narrow land the anchor's site may lie a few hundred kilometres from it (Leang Karampuang's lies about 220 km up Sulawesi's south-western arm, which is too narrow for any camp, sites.md section 2).
- **Clear ground.** No tree stands in a scene (issues 171, 173: Lascaux's wall and Mezhyrich's hut had trees growing through them): the trees make way round the prop as it rises, within 22 layout metres of a cave wall (the cut itself reaches 13 each way, and a tree beside either end would hide the picture from the camp: Yang, issue 171 follow-up), 8 of a grave, 16 of a bone hut and 20 of a ring (all at the camp's scale), as they do for a camp.

Which windows they are depends only on the anchor and the camp, never on who is free, so the painting grows the same whatever happens at the camp and every time window's chunk agrees. In a session window the camp has no catch or dance. Its people are in place 5 seconds of watching into the window and paint or bury for 9 seconds, so they are back before it ends; each place at the wall or grave goes to the next of them who can be there in time and back by the window's end. The build checks that every session has its people (a painter, or two who dig), so nothing happens by itself.
- **The cave.** A hill is cut in half (Yang, 2026-10-21: "Instead of the ground rising, we should do the opposite. On an existing small hill, half the hill's terrain lowers so that there is a rock face between the halves. People can walk down the lowered side to paint on the rock face"; until then the ground rose into a hill with an alcove): the scene stands where its cut and 16 layout metres round it are clear of rivers, lakes and the sea, where its camp has such a place (Yang, 2026-10-21: "move the cave away from the water. otherwise it feels like the water must flood the cave"), then as far out from the camp as it fits and where the ground rises most from the camp, so it is on a hill where there is one near. Leang Karampuang: a chunky warty pig with its long snout, warts, ears, bristly back, stubby legs and curly tail, three little hunters round it holding sticks to it, and two hand stencils (after Oktaviana et al. 2024); Chauvet: four horses' heads one above another with spiky manes, an aurochs with lyre horns, two woolly rhinos facing each other with their long nose horns, two lions' heads and a mammoth (after Clottes 2003); Lascaux, in white calcite rock, big black and red-brown bulls, a red cow, little horses, a stag with great antlers and rows of dots (after Aujoulat 2004). ### 8.3 Site cards

Yang asked on 2026-10-21: "Landmarks should have cards. Create them for the caves and the burial. Also prepare one for Göbekli Tepe. Clicking on the landmark brings up the card." Every anchor (sites.md section 4.1) has a **site card**, a card like an animal's (wildlife.md section 8) about the real place.

- **Records.** `data/curated/site-cards/<anchor id>.md`, its id equal to the anchor's. The front matter holds `id`, `kind: site-card` and `sources` (references into `sources.yaml`, at least one); the body has `## en` and `## de` sections. The data build fails when an anchor has no card, a card has no anchor, or a record breaks the envelope rules of content-format.md section 2.
- **Structure.** Each language section starts with `# Name`, then in this order: two lines `**Made:**` and `**Discovered:**` (`**Entstanden:**`, `**Entdeckt:**`) saying when it was made and when and how it was found, then the sub-headings `### What was found`, `### How it looked` and `### What it was for` (`### Was man fand`, `### So sah es aus`, `### Wozu es diente`), in that order and no other. "How it looked" says how it probably looked when it was new, and what is uncertain; "What it was for" says what is known and names what is debated. Dates before 1500 are marked approximate. Written for a reader of about 11 to 12, nothing bloody, English and German saying the same.
- **Opening.** Tapping a scene's wall or grave (section 8.2) opens its anchor's card, in the panel at the top left, never pausing time. The tap is tested before the animals: the nearest drawn scene prop whose middle lies within its drawn size on screen (at least 28 CSS pixels). Göbekli Tepe's ring is such a prop, so its card opens the same way while the ring stands, once the ring model is approved.
- **Loading.** The startup index carries each card's id and title (`siteCards`); the text is one small file per card and language, loaded when the card opens.

### 8.5 Neolithic villages

Where villages stand, how they stay and how they are laid out is in [neolithic/villages.md](neolithic/villages.md).

- **Culture.** Where the farming spread has reached a place with crops (`farmingStateAt(...).stage === 'farming'`) and a Neolithic profile applies there ([neolithic/farming-spread.md](neolithic/farming-spread.md) section 9.4), the build gives the camps there that profile as their culture, over the culture area's. - **Houses.** The ring is 12 layout metres and has 5 slots; the Neolithic houses are drawn at most 2.7 layout metres wide (`LODGING_MAX_M`), so five never touch (a 2.7 m house on a 12 m ring has about 15 m between neighbours' middles). A test builds every profile's house at every slot and checks that no two footprints overlap and none reaches the hearth.
- **Herding stage.** Between the onset and the first crops, camps keep their hunter-gatherer culture but the build marks lodgings built then `herding`; such a camp draws 3 to 6 herd animals of its region's herd (the nearest profile's `herd`, else cattle and goats) grazing and standing about 16 to 22 layout metres out in a loose group, and one `prop-pen` beside them. No fields.

The scene is terrain-free, so the build records for each profile lodging a **land mask** `fieldLand`: a bit per cell of 48 sectors times 10 rings (10 to 37 layout metres) that is 1 where the ground is dry at the sea level of the date, off rivers (the camps' own river bank test) and within 300 m of the hearth's height; the fields keep to cells that are 1, so none lies on water, a river or a mountain. The fields are the same for every viewer: the seed is the camp's site key.
- **Crops.** Each field's crop rotates through the profile's crops (its `crops` in the culture table `cultures.json`), its stage is `fieldStage` of the village's life clock: a field changes only when it is worked (one job per 30 s slot, the fields in turn, an assigned farmer doing it), and an unworked field holds still ([neolithic/fields-and-crops.md](neolithic/fields-and-crops.md) section 2; Yang, 2026-10-24: "crops grow too quickly"). The crops are instanced (`cropGeometry` for the stage's keyframe, the continuous part as the Y scale), without ink; tufts on a jittered grid inside the outline in the furrow frame, at `CROP_SPACING_M` but never more than about 220 to a field (the spacing grows for a big field), each drawn 3 times as wide as its spacing so the rows close up; they stand on the same lifted patch as the ground, and the instances are cached per field and rebuilt only when a stage step or the ground changes; a village seen from afar draws every second, then every fourth tuft, and none beyond 6,000 km altitude. `FieldGround` paints the tilled patches.
- **Activities.** The roles farmer, reaper, herder, potter, weaver, builder and grinder (their German nouns in feminine and masculine form are in `app/src/world/roles.ts`, the vocabulary of people-profiles.md section 4) join the activity mix of a profile's grown-ups with the shares of that table. Outings: **till**, **sow** and weeding (drawn as till) and **reap** with **thresh** (the field's next job in its work cycle, done by the assigned farmer or reaper, then at the threshing spot beside the ring), **grind**, **milk**, **shape** and **spin** (at spots beside the person's own lodging, as the nut-cracker's). The person walks from their spot to the field's edge (`go`), works, and walks back, exactly as a gatherer does (section 8.1): nobody appears anywhere. Which field, and where on it, is chosen by hash of the figure and the turn. If the land mask leaves no field, the farmer gathers instead.
- **Herd scale and wild animals (Yang, 2026-10-23: black cattle, animals too large).** The village herd is drawn at 0.6 of life size (`HERD_SCALE`): sheep and goats knee-high to a person, cattle about waist-high, in step with the storybook houses. Wild animals keep 50 layout metres from a camp's hearth (`VILLAGE_KEEP_OUT_KM`), so none wanders through a pen or a field. The wild aurochs is dark brown (#4a3424), recoloured from black at Yang's request, 2026-10-24.
- **Held tools.** Away from a hunt, a figure on a work outing holds the tool of its clip (`CLIP_TOOL`, `world/scene/camp.ts`): hoe or digging-stick for till (the profile's field tool), basket for sow, sickle for reap, crook for thresh, grinding-stone for grind, pot for shape, spindle for spin; milking holds nothing. Models are built per tool, as for the spear.
- **Herd first, fields close (Yang, 2026-10-24: "fields overlap with animal pastures", "fields should be much closer to the living quarters. also, consider no longer having a fireplace").** The pen and its 3 to 6 animals are placed first; fields keep `PEN_CLEAR_M` = 8 m from the pen's middle and `HERD_FIELD_CLEAR_M` = 2.5 m from each animal, and sit as near the lodging ring as the ring allows (nearest edge about 14 m from the hearth). The fireplace is dropped, see **No fire in a village** below.
- **Lanes, trees, tufts (Yang, 2026-10-23).** Fields and the pen keep out of the camp's outing lanes (`inOutingLane`: the five gaps between the lodgings, a lane's width either side of the walks, section 8.1), so no outing, hunt or hunted animal crosses a crop. The trees keep a field's farthest corner plus 2.5 layout metres clear. From high up the tufts are thinned to every second, fourth or eighth and are not enlarged, so a field keeps its size at every zoom (Yang, 2026-10-25; until then each tuft kept was drawn larger by the square root of the thinning). A field's drawn crop stage moves by one of the five stages at a time and at most every 0.7 seconds of drawing, so a clock running through years does not make it flicker. A camp of any size, compact and minimal ones too, has fields where the farming state says farming; its lodging stays its layout's.
- **No fire in a village (Yang, 2026-10-24: "drop the fireplace for neolithic. the transition from paleolithic to neolithic is that the band packs up the camp and builds neolithic houses, and start to build fields").** The change of culture is the pack-up of section 8.1 and sites.md section 5: the band takes its hunter-gatherer lodgings down and builds the profile's houses in the same place, and starts its fields. The hearth burns only while a hunter-gatherer (or herding-stage) lodging of its place stands (`Place.fireTo`); once the last is down there is no fire, and a village founded as one has none and no one lights one with flint. The mask rings, lanes and spots stay as they were; the middle of the ring is an empty green that stays clear of paths (`FIRE_CLEAR_M`), where the catch and dancing events still take place. What of the hunter-gatherer day still makes sense: gathering, hunting (with the shares of the profile's table), fetching water, fishing, firewood (for the houses), scraping and hanging hides, making spears and flint tools, strolling, the hut, catch and dance. What does not: tending the fire, cracking nuts by it and roasting a kill on a spit; those turns are rest, standing or sitting, instead. A stand-in for the fire in the middle (a well or a tree) would be a new prop, which needs Yang's approval, and is not drawn yet.
- **Tending (Yang, 2026-10-24: "field need to be tended to in order to grow").** As in fields-and-crops.md section 2, a field's stage moves only when its slot's job is done: the village's worker for the slot (a farmer or reaper of the village at home, else any grown-up at home) walks to the field and does it. A village too small or empty of grown-ups at a slot leaves the field as it is, which is why a village grows to 6 to 10 people (section 4.1).
- **Performance.** The fields are worked out once per camp stay (memoised with the chunk); tufts are capped at 2,500 per village; the scene tests' time and size budgets stay.
