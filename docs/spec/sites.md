# Camp sites

This is the design of the places where the bands of the tree of humankind ([humankind.md](humankind.md)) set up camp, how those places connect, and the rules that decide which bands live where, when they move and what they become. It replaces the free search for camp spots of humankind.md section 4 and the route bands of [peopling.md](peopling.md#9-order-of-work) section 9.1. Where they disagree, fix whichever is wrong in the same pull request. Decisions marked **Proposed** are defaults chosen so work can start and are open for Yang to change.

Asked for by Yang on 2026-10-19, after many rounds of tuning the free search to keep people out of the water: "a precomputed list of sites, how they connect to each other, and a predefining how bands populate them deterministically, makes a lot of sense. We would then also be able to predefine how a band morphs into a different band or civilization. Make sure when people migrate from one site to another, they don't walk over water. And that each site is large enough to have space for all activities. And also coordinate with foliage growth." And earlier the same day: "we will need rules bound to region and time anyhow, once we want to re-enact historical events. E.g. a tribe morphs into the Egyptians."

## 1. The idea in one paragraph

The data build lays a fixed lattice of **sites** over the land: places a camp can stand, each with its own layout of hearth, people's spots, lodging places, racks and outing lanes. For every site it works out, once, the sea level at which any part of that layout would be under water, so whether a site has room for all of its activities at a date is a simple comparison with the sea level of the date. Sites are joined by **links** where the way between them is land at a sea level, or a listed sea crossing by canoe. Bands only ever stand at sites and only ever travel along links, so nobody camps, works or walks in the water by construction, not by after-the-fact checks. Which sites are taken, by whom and when, is decided by **rules bound to a region and a time**: the culture areas with their densities and limits, the arrival map, the Neolithic thresholds, and **morphs** that turn one culture into another, such as hunter-gatherers of the Nile into the first Egyptians.

## 2. Sites

- **The lattice.** Candidate sites lie on rows of latitude about 225 km apart (**Proposed**; 450 km until Yang asked on 2026-10-19 to "allow overlapping sites ... at least allow nomads to roam a bit"), each row's points about 225 km apart along it and every other row shifted by half a step, from 60°S to 80°N. Within its cell of the lattice each site takes the place where its camp fits best, of a 3 by 3 grid across the cell. A candidate is kept when its camp fits at the lowest sea level of the timeline. A culture area may ask for a coarser lattice (`siteStepKm` in its record, a multiple of the step): Siberia's two areas have one of 450 km, keeping every other row and every other point along it, since few bands lived there (Yang, 2026-10-19: "let's have a less dense mesh of sites there"). Key places keep their sites wherever they are. Sites overlap: their drawn camps are far larger than the lattice step.
- **Overlapping sites are mutually exclusive.** Two bands never stand at sites whose drawn camps would overlap (their footprints, out to the huts and racks: 18 layout metres round a full camp's hearth, 13.6 round a compact one's and 12.4 round a minimal one's, about 510, 385 and 350 km), and as before no closer than the camp spacing of their density (section 4).
- **Key places** (Yang, 2026-10-19: "important historical places (rome, athens, carthage, london) absolutely need a site"). A curated list, `data/curated/key-places.json`, names the places history will need a camp, village or town at, each with a source. Each gets a site within 300 km of it, or 450 km where nothing fits nearer (**Proposed**), whose camp stays dry even at the highest sea level of the timeline, so it can be used in every age, the largest that fits there: a full camp, a compact one, or, where even that does not fit, a **minimal** one: one small brush shelter for one family (the new model `shelter-small-hut`, about 1.7 m across, whatever the culture; Yang, 2026-10-19: "let's choose a different hut design for these places") 6.2 layout metres from the fire, its door 3.1 metres in front of it, and at most four people close round the fire (3.1 metres out), whose core is 3.6 metres round the hearth. Sites are checked as the globe draws them through the history lens at its fixed 120% (history-lens.md section 1): a camp's layout covers fewer true kilometres where the lens draws the land bigger, so the busy regions it enlarges, such as Italy and Greece, have room for camps. With that, and everything drawn 15% smaller, Rome, Athens and the other key places get sites; Kyoto has none for now, Honshu being too narrow even for a minimal camp. The build fails if a key place has no site at all. Each site has a fixed id, place and layout (the turn of its ring of lodging places, from the hash of its id), which every camp there uses.
- **Room for all activities.** A site's **core** is the disk round its hearth out past the outer ring of people's spots, where people stand, work, dance and play catch (out to 9.5 layout metres, about 270 km at drawn size), checked on rings every 0.75 layout metres, 64 points each. Its five **lodging places** each need the lodging, its door, the builders round it, its drying rack and the place where hides are scraped (humankind.md section 8.1). Its five **outing lanes**, one per gap between the lodging places, are measured out to 50 layout metres, every half metre, on rays across the lane, every 0.025 radians.
- **Flood levels.** For every point checked, the build takes the sea level above which the point is sea (from the source grids and the drawn tiles, whichever floods first, milestone 1 section 5.1); points in a lake or on a drawn river and its bank are never dry. The site keeps the lowest of these over its core (the **core level**), over each lodging place (a **place level** each), and along each lane (the lane's level at each distance). At a sea level below the core level the site has room for its people; a lodging place can stand where the sea is below its level; a lane reaches as far out as its levels stay above the sea.
- **When a site can be used.** A band may camp at a site while, up to its next move (two minutes of watching ahead, the longest residence window), the sea stays below the core level and the levels of at least two lodging places (a band of three to five needs two lodgings), and the hearth is habitable for the culture there (land not under ice, not desert, not too high or cold, peopling.md section 5.2). Lodgings take only places whose level stays above the sea for that time, and record the lanes' reach for it, so outings stay on land.
- **Compact camps** (Yang, 2026-10-19: "adapt camps for undersized sites where absolutely needed"). Where no full site fits in a cell of the lattice, the build tries a compact one: its huts stand 9 layout metres from the fire instead of 12, their doors 4.6 metres in front of them, its builders behind them, its people on one ring 3.8 metres out, eight at most, with no drying racks and so no hide scraping, no catch, and dancing close round the fire (3.4 metres); its core is 5 metres round the hearth, and one lodging place is enough for up to four people. Compact sites carry ids from 1,000,000 on and minimal ones from 2,000,000 on, so the scene knows the layout from a camp's id alone. Bands take one only where no full site is free, which is mostly as the sea cuts up Sundaland, and on narrow land such as the Levant and the Malay peninsula.
- **Too small to camp.** Land where not even a compact site has room is not camped on: narrow coasts, small islands and peninsulas such as Italy or Japan at high sea level. Bands pass along them where links allow, and the build reports every arrival point whose first camp comes more than 500 years after its date (peopling.md section 4), so such places can be looked at.

## 3. Links

- **On foot.** Two sites at most 675 km apart (**Proposed**, three lattice steps), and each site with its nearest site in each of eight directions beyond that up to 2,500 km, so a band can pass along land too narrow for any site (the Malay peninsula), are linked when the straight way between their hearths is land, checked every 10 km along it and 15 km either side, since walkers are drawn a little off the line. Lakes block a link; rivers are forded. Like a site, a link keeps the lowest flood level along it, so it can be walked while the sea is below it: the Bering land bridge and the Sunda shelf open and close with the sea.
- **Waypoints.** On land farther than 300 km from every site, the build lays a waypoint about every 100 km, which bands walk through but never camp at, linked to everything within 600 km of it over land. So land too narrow or broken for any site, such as the isthmus of Panama, still joins the sites on either side.
- **By canoe.** Each listed crossing (peopling.md section 6) links the three sites within 900 km of its first shore that stay dry the longest with the three such sites of its far shore, each taken only from sites nearer its own shore than the other (until 2026-10-19 both ends of the Korea–Japan crossing could fall in Korea, so no band reached Japan). From the crossing's date, a band crossing it walks to the shore, paddles along the crossing's way and walks on, as the scene already shows.
- **Stranded.** A band whose site is cut off by the rising sea, with no free site it can walk to, may paddle straight to the nearest site within 3,600 km (**Proposed**), putting in and landing wherever the way changes between land and water: to a free site, to a band there with dry places free for its lodgings, or, last of all, to any empty site it can use however near another band (the build counts these): people of the coasts had boats.
- **Paths.** Every move of a band is a path of links from site to site, worked out by the build and stored with the band's new lodging, so the scene walks it leg by leg and paddles where a leg crosses water.

## 4. How bands populate the sites

All of it is deterministic: whole days, the seeded hash and the build's own maths (humankind.md section 5).

- **Taken sites.** A site may hold one band. No two bands stand closer than the camp spacing of the density at the site (humankind.md section 4.1), so their drawn camps do not overlap.
- **Moving.** At the end of each residence window a band moves along one link of at most 400 km to a free usable site (it roams; Yang, 2026-10-19), the best by the habitat score of humankind.md section 4.3 (near water, open ground, low and green) with a little hash, or stays where no neighbour is free. A settled band stays.
- **The water comes.** When its site, or the lodging places it uses, will not stay dry until its next move, the band leaves first, to the nearest free usable site along links; failing that, it joins nearby bands with room, splitting up if it must (at most twenty people to a full camp, both its rings of spots, and eight to a compact one), or, last of all, takes any empty site it can use however near another band; failing that, it paddles away as a stranded band (section 3). Nobody is lost to the sea.
- **Budding.** A full camp sends two or three people to the best free usable site the rules allow, at least one camp spacing from every other band and at most five links away (**Proposed**).
- **Following a route** (planned legs; Yang, 2026-10-19: "can't you predefine which band needs to traverse which sites to reach japan?", after a change in Siberia left Japan unreached). Each leg of a route, from one arrival point to the next (peopling.md section 3), is planned from the geography alone, not from where other bands happen to be. Its **target** is the site nearest the arrival point that a camp of the point's culture can use on the leg's day (dry and habitable, section 2), on the point's own land (it can walk, over open links that are not canoe crossings, to the leg's planned target, the site its culture can use nearest the point on the leg's day, where that is within 1,000 km of the point, or else to the site nearest the point; so a site in Korea does not count for Japan), and not overlapped by a standing camp: the nearest such site that also keeps the spacing of section 4 to every band, or, where none does, the nearest one at all, since the route is history. Where that site is more than 1,000 km from the point (the land near it not yet open, such as the ice-free corridor while the ice retreats), the leg waits for a nearer one, at most 500 years, before it goes to the far one. Its **origin** is the previous leg's target, or none for a leg out of Africa. Once the point's date has come and no band of the point's culture stands within 1,000 km of it on its own land (a band in Korea does not count for Japan), the band at the origin, or failing that the band nearest the origin along links with people to spare, sends a group of two or three. It walks to the origin, then along the shortest path of open links from the origin to the target, across a listed crossing where the route crosses the sea, and founds a camp there. While no such path is open on the day (a land bridge under water), the band nearest the target along open links goes instead, and while there is none the leg waits and tries again. A leg without an origin, or whose point has no usable site within 3,000 km, uses the search of before: the best free usable site within a camp spacing of the point (in a `remote` area no farther than 1,000 km), and the band nearest it along links.
- **Band caps** (Yang, 2026-10-19: a hybrid of rules and a table, after the counts of bands in Siberia, South America and Australia kept shifting whenever any rule changed). A culture area may cap its bands: `maxBands` in its record, a list of `[bce, count]` pairs, each holding from its year until the next. A band does not bud into a site of a full area, and a band fleeing the sea or the desert goes to an area with room, or joins a band nearby, before it moves into a full one. Bands placed by routes (below) and anchors (section 4.1) count towards the cap but are placed even when the area is full, since they are history. Siberia has 1 (with the bands its route places at its three arrival points, about 3), Beringia east of the date line 1, Australia with Tasmania 3 and South America 10 (**Proposed**, from Yang's counts of 2026-10-19).
- **Home ranges.** When a band is founded it takes the site it founds and up to three usable sites linked to it within the roaming distance of 400 km, nearest first, that no other band holds: its home range. It roams only among them, and no other band buds onto them, so a band's comings and goings do not push others around. A band that must flee the sea (above), and a band that a route or anchor moves, takes a new home range where it lands; the sites of its old one become free.
- **The migration table.** The routes of the arrival map (peopling.md section 4, `data/curated/arrivals.json`) are the table of high-level migrations: who goes where and when, from point to point in order. A route keeps its own band: the band founded by a leg sends the group of the next leg, if it still stands and has people to spare; otherwise the band at the leg's origin, then the band nearest it, as below.
- **Rules bound to region and time.** Which sites may be used, at what density, by which culture and with which limits, comes from the rule records: the culture areas (humankind.md section 5, peopling.md section 5), the arrival map (peopling.md section 4), the Neolithic thresholds (peopling.md section 3.6), and morphs (section 5). They are the only place where history is put into the tree; the build has no other special cases.

### 4.1 Anchors

Later milestones need a band at an exact place and time: the camp that becomes the first village at the Nile before the Egyptians, or the band at the Palatine hill when Rome is founded (Yang, 2026-10-19: "I need to be able to specify exact time and position at which a site needs to have a band so that they can morph into a civilization like Egypt, or when Rome is founded"). An **anchor** in `data/curated/anchors.json` names one:

| Field | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `id` | The anchor, also used by the records that need it (a morph, a settlement, a vignette) |
| `bce` | The year from which a band must stand there (or `ce`) |
| `lat`, `lon` | The place: the anchor's site is the camp site nearest it, within 300 km, that a camp can use on that day (dry and habitable for the culture there) |
| `until` | Optional: the year to which the band stays; without it, to the end of the tree |
| `en`, `de`, `source` | What it is, in both languages, and the source of its date and place |

The build makes it so:

- **The site.** Like a key place (section 2), each anchor gets a camp site laid near it, within 75 km where any layout fits (else as near as a key place's), dry at every sea level. On the anchor's day the site nearest the place that is usable, within 300 km, is the anchor's site; without one the build fails naming the anchor.
- **Getting there.** From 500 years before the day (**Proposed**: long enough to walk there from a neighbouring region), if no band stands at the site, the band nearest it along links with people to spare sends a group of two or three by the shortest open path, as a route's leg does; a camp that stands where the site's camp would overlap moves onto the site instead. It tries again at each step until a band stands there.
- **Staying.** From its arrival until `until`, the band at the anchor's site is settled: it does not roam or bud, and its home range is the site alone. It still flees the sea; an anchor whose site the sea would flood within its span fails the build.
- **Checked.** The build fails when, on an anchor's day, no band stands at its site, naming the anchor and the nearest band, so a wrong date or place shows at once. The checks of section 7 confirm that the band stays until `until`.
- **For now** the tree ends about 6500 BCE (peopling.md section 3.5), so anchors of later dates wait for the tree to run on. The first anchor is Göbekli Tepe, where hunter-gatherers raised the stone enclosures from about 9500 BCE (Schmidt 2010, *Göbekli Tepe: the Stone Age sanctuaries*, Documenta Praehistorica 37), so the vignette of its building (milestone-2 section 8) always has people there.

## 5. Morphs

A **morph** is a rule that, from a date, the bands in a region take on another culture: hunter-gatherers of the Levant becoming Natufians about 12,500 BCE, or, in milestone 3, foragers of the Nile valley becoming the farmers and then the towns of predynastic and dynastic Egypt. A culture area that starts on a date where another one was is a morph. Each band in the region changes once, at a moment chosen by hash within the morph's transition window (100 years, **Proposed**, as spec section 9 has eras morph gradually): it rebuilds its lodgings in the new culture's kind where it stands, and its people go into their huts and come out in the new clothes (peopling.md section 5.3). A band moving into a region with another culture changes the same way when it arrives. Morphs into settlements (towns, palaces, forts) come with milestone 3 and use the same records.

## 6. Foliage

Trees and camps share the ground (humankind.md section 7, [foliage.md](foliage.md) section 4). Each site remembers when its last band left. A band arriving fells only the trees that have grown back since: all of them after the regrowth time (five years, about 12 seconds of watching), a share of them before it, none if it returns at once; the felling takes as long as there is to fell. While a band stands at a site the trees stay cleared there, and when it leaves they grow back over the regrowth time, starting from when it left, so a camp's clearing and the forest around it always agree, whichever band comes next.

## 7. Build, tests and tools

- **Stage.** `data/build/stages/sites.ts` (**Proposed**) builds the sites and links after the climate stage and before the tree, writes them to `data/build/tree/sites.json` (git-ignored) for the tools, and logs how many sites and links there are and how many are usable at a few dates. The sites take about a minute to lay out, so the build keeps them in `data/out/sites/` (git-ignored) by a hash of everything they depend on: the source data, the history lens, the key places, the crossings, the coarser lattices of the culture areas, the camp layouts and the build code that makes the land and the sites. Tuning the tree (densities, spacings, routes, morphs) then rebuilds only the tree. Only the newest set is kept.
- **Checks** (failing the build): every lodging stands at a usable site and place on the day it is built and until it is taken down; every walked leg of a band's path is a link usable on the day it is walked; no two bands stand closer than their spacing.
- **Tests.** Unit tests on a small synthetic world: a site whose core floods is not used; a band on a shrinking island leaves before the water reaches it; a land bridge link opens and closes with the sea; a morph changes each band once, inside its hut; a band returning to a site fells only what has regrown. The water test on the full data (milestone 1 section 9) keeps checking every figure, prey and lodging the scene shows.
- **Development view** (Yang, 2026-10-19: "add a debug option to show the lattice"). With `?sites` in the address, the globe shows every site as a dot, green for a full camp, yellow for a compact one, orange for a minimal one and pale for a waypoint, dimmed to dark blue while the sea at the clock's date covers it, and every link as a line, bright while it can be walked or paddled. The build writes the sites and links for it as one small file, which the app loads only with the flag.
