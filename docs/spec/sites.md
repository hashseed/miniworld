# Camp sites

This is the design of the places where the bands of the tree of humankind ([humankind.md](humankind.md)) set up camp, how those places connect, and the rules that decide which bands live where, when they move and what they become. It replaces the free search for camp spots of humankind.md section 4 and the route bands of [peopling.md](peopling.md#9-order-of-work) section 9.1. Where they disagree, fix whichever is wrong in the same pull request. Decisions marked **Proposed** are defaults chosen so work can start and are open for Yang to change.

Asked for by Yang on 2026-10-19, after many rounds of tuning the free search to keep people out of the water: "a precomputed list of sites, how they connect to each other, and a predefining how bands populate them deterministically, makes a lot of sense. We would then also be able to predefine how a band morphs into a different band or civilization. Make sure when people migrate from one site to another, they don't walk over water. And that each site is large enough to have space for all activities. And also coordinate with foliage growth." And earlier the same day: "we will need rules bound to region and time anyhow, once we want to re-enact historical events. E.g. a tribe morphs into the Egyptians."

## 1. The idea in one paragraph

The data build lays a fixed lattice of **sites** over the land: places a camp can stand, each with its own layout of hearth, people's spots, lodging places, racks and outing lanes. For every site it works out, once, the sea level at which any part of that layout would be under water, so whether a site has room for all of its activities at a date is a simple comparison with the sea level of the date. Sites are joined by **links** where the way between them is land at a sea level, or a listed sea crossing by canoe. Bands only ever stand at sites and only ever travel along links, so nobody camps, works or walks in the water by construction, not by after-the-fact checks. Which sites are taken, by whom and when, is decided by **rules bound to a region and a time**: the culture areas with their densities and limits, the arrival map, the Neolithic thresholds, and **morphs** that turn one culture into another, such as hunter-gatherers of the Nile into the first Egyptians.

## 2. Sites

- **The lattice.** Candidate sites lie on rows of latitude about 225 km apart (**Proposed**; 450 km until Yang asked on 2026-10-19 to "allow overlapping sites ... at least allow nomads to roam a bit"), each row's points about 225 km apart along it and every other row shifted by half a step, from 60°S to 80°N. Within its cell of the lattice each site takes the place where its camp fits best, of a 3 by 3 grid across the cell. A candidate is kept when its camp fits at the lowest sea level of the timeline. A culture area may ask for a coarser lattice (`siteStepKm` in its record, a multiple of the step): Siberia's two areas have one of 450 km, keeping every other row and every other point along it, since few bands lived there (Yang, 2026-10-19: "let's have a less dense mesh of sites there"). Key places keep their sites wherever they are. Sites overlap: their drawn camps are far larger than the lattice step.
- **Overlapping sites are mutually exclusive.** Two bands never stand at sites whose drawn camps would overlap (their footprints, out to the huts and racks: 18 layout metres round a full camp's hearth, 13.6 round a compact one's and 12.4 round a minimal one's, about 510, 385 and 350 km), and as before no closer than the camp spacing of their density (section 4).
- **Key places** (Yang, 2026-10-19: "important historical places (rome, athens, carthage, london) absolutely need a site"). A curated list, `data/curated/key-places.json`, names the places history will need a camp, village or town at, each with a source. Each gets a site within 300 km of it, or 450 km where nothing fits nearer (**Proposed**), whose camp stays dry even at the highest sea level of the timeline, so it can be used in every age, the largest that fits there: a full camp, a compact one, or, where even that does not fit, a **minimal** one: one small brush shelter for one family (the new model `shelter-small-hut`, about 1.7 m across, whatever the culture; Yang, 2026-10-19: "let's choose a different hut design for these places") 6.2 layout metres from the fire, its door 3.1 metres in front of it, and at most four people close round the fire (3.1 metres out), whose core is 3.6 metres round the hearth. Sites are checked as the globe draws them through the history lens at its fixed 120% (history-lens.md section 1): a camp's layout covers fewer true kilometres where the lens draws the land bigger, so the busy regions it enlarges, such as Italy and Greece, have room for camps. With that, and everything drawn 15% smaller, Rome, Athens and the other key places get sites; Kyoto has none for now, Honshu being too narrow even for a minimal camp. The build fails if a key place has no site at all. Each site has a fixed id, place and layout (the turn of its ring of lodging places, from the hash of its id), which every camp there uses.
- **Room for all activities.** A site's **core** is the disk round its hearth out past the outer ring of people's spots, where people stand, work, dance and play catch (out to 9.5 layout metres, about 270 km at drawn size), checked on rings every 0.75 layout metres, 64 points each. Its five **lodging places** each need the lodging, its door, the builders round it, its drying rack and the place where hides are scraped (humankind.md section 8.1). Its five **outing lanes**, one per gap between the lodging places, are measured out to 50 layout metres, every half metre, on rays across the lane, every 0.025 radians.
- **Flood levels.** For every point checked, the build takes the sea level above which the point is sea (from the source grids and the drawn tiles, whichever floods first, milestone 1 section 5.1); points in a lake or on a drawn river and its bank are never dry. The site keeps the lowest of these over its core (the **core level**), over each lodging place (a **place level** each), and along each lane (the lane's level at each distance). At a sea level below the core level the site has room for its people; a lodging place can stand where the sea is below its level; a lane reaches as far out as its levels stay above the sea.
- **Even ground** (Yang, 2026-10-20: "It doesn't make sense to have the fire place in the india and the hut on the himalaya"). A layout metre is about 24 km of land, so a camp spans hundreds of kilometres and a mountain front can run through it. A lodging place whose ground (at any point checked for it) lies more than 1,500 m above or below the hearth is no place: its level is taken as always flooded, so no lodging stands there and huts go on the side of the fire where the land is level. Within its cell, a site prefers a place where its ground rises and falls at most 1,500 m between the hearth, the edge of its core and its lodging places (its **relief**): a full camp there, else a compact one (Yang, 2026-10-20: "Or use compact camps"), whose huts 9 layout metres out reach less of a mountain front, as long as it stays dry as long as the best uneven place would, up to the highest sea; else, in the same order, the evenest place that does, not the highest. Key places try even ground in every layout, full, compact and minimal, then the evenest ground within their radius. A key place that still gets no site within its radius takes one there whose lodging places may lie farther above or below the hearth, before looking farther out (Sulawesi's steep arms have almost no place within 1,500 m of a hearth). The quick first ranking of a cell's places applies the same rule to lodging places as the full check. Children do not play catch at a camp whose ground rises or falls more than 600 m out to the catch ring (humankind.md section 8.1).
- **When a site can be used.** A band may camp at a site while, up to its next move (two minutes of watching ahead, the longest residence window), the sea stays below the core level and the levels of at least two lodging places (a band of three to five needs two lodgings), and the hearth is habitable for the culture there (land not under ice, not desert, not too high or cold, peopling.md section 5.2). Lodgings take only places whose level stays above the sea for that time, and record the lanes' reach for it, so outings stay on land.
- **Compact camps** (Yang, 2026-10-19: "adapt camps for undersized sites where absolutely needed"). Where no full site fits in a cell of the lattice, the build tries a compact one: its huts stand 9 layout metres from the fire instead of 12, their doors 4.6 metres in front of them, its builders behind them, its people on one ring 3.8 metres out, eight at most, with no drying racks and so no hide scraping, no catch, and dancing close round the fire (3.4 metres); its core is 5 metres round the hearth, and one lodging place is enough for up to four people. Compact sites carry ids from 1,000,000 on and minimal ones from 2,000,000 on, so the scene knows the layout from a camp's id alone. Bands take one only where no full site is free, which is mostly as the sea cuts up Sundaland, and on narrow land such as the Levant and the Malay peninsula.
- **Too small to camp.** Land where not even a compact site has room is not camped on: narrow coasts, small islands and peninsulas such as Italy or Japan at high sea level. Bands pass along them where links allow, and the build reports every arrival point whose first camp comes more than 500 years after its date (peopling.md section 4), so such places can be looked at.

### 2.1 The place table

Yang, 2026-10-20: "I think we first should map historical locations to our generated list of camp sites as a pre-step", after the events dialog (milestone-1 section 8.3) showed route points counted as reached by bands up to 2,600 km away, and points such as Iberia and Japan whose nearest camps stood across the sea. The build of the tree is a solver of constraints: records that need a band at a place at a time. Before the tree runs, every such record gets **its own site**, once, from the geography and its date alone, never from where bands happen to stand. The tree then works only with these sites. There will be hundreds or thousands of such records, so this step is cheap per record and fails early, naming the record.

- **Dated places.** Each arrival point of the routes that is camped at (peopling.md section 4; not a passage), each anchor (section 4.1) and each Neolithic threshold (peopling.md section 3.6). Each has a day and a **span**, the time its site must be usable: for an arrival point its date and the 500 years after it (the longest a leg waits); for an anchor its year to its `until`; for a threshold its date to the tree's end. Later records that need a band (events of scenes, morphs into settlements) join the same table.
- **Laying.** As for a key place (section 2), the site stage lays a site for each dated place within 300 km of it where none is, or within 450 km where nothing fits nearer; here the site must stay dry at the highest sea of the span, not of the whole timeline. An anchor's site is laid within 75 km where any layout fits, as before.
- **Choosing.** The place's site is the site nearest the place, within 300 km, else within 450 km, that on the place's day (for an arrival point, the day the arrival map reaches the site, if that falls within the span) is usable by a camp of the place's culture for a residence window (section 2: dry and habitable), stays dry for the span, and lies on the place's own land: it walks, within 24 links open on the day and not by canoe, to the place's **home**: the site or waypoint nearest the place whose straight way to it is land on the day, checked every 10 km along it as a link is (section 3) but not either side of it, which only walkers drawn off the line need (2026-10-20: Leang Karampuang, a few kilometres from Sulawesi's coast, had no home with the sides checked). So a site in Korea never stands for Japan, and a place on land without any site or waypoint has no site. Ties go to the lower site id.
- **Failing.** A dated place with no such site fails the build, naming the record, the nearest site and why it does not do (flooded, not habitable, across water, too far).
- **The table.** The build writes the table to `data/build/tree/places.json` (git-ignored): per record its kind, id, day, the site's id and position and its distance from the place, and logs how many places there are and the farthest. The events dialog (milestone-1 section 8.3) and the tree read their sites from it.
- **Key places** keep their rule of section 2 (a site dry in every age); they need no band, so they are not in the table.

## 3. Links

- **On foot.** Two sites at most 675 km apart (**Proposed**, three lattice steps), and each site with its nearest site in each of eight directions beyond that up to 2,500 km, so a band can pass along land too narrow for any site (the Malay peninsula), are linked when the straight way between their hearths is land, checked every 10 km along it and 15 km either side, since walkers are drawn a little off the line. Lakes block a link; rivers are forded. Like a site, a link keeps the lowest flood level along it, so it can be walked while the sea is below it: the Bering land bridge and the Sunda shelf open and close with the sea.
- **Waypoints.** On land farther than 300 km from every site, the build lays a waypoint about every 100 km, which bands walk through but never camp at, linked to everything within 600 km of it over land. So land too narrow or broken for any site, such as the isthmus of Panama, still joins the sites on either side.
- **By canoe.** Each listed crossing (peopling.md section 6) links the three sites within 900 km of its first shore that stay dry the longest with the three such sites of its far shore, each taken only from its own shore's land: sites within 900 km of the shore that walk, at the lowest sea of the timeline, to the shore's home (the camp site nearest it whose straight way to it is land, the last 25 km before the shore point, which may lie just off the drawn coast, not checked); a crossing whose shore has none (New Ireland, too narrow for any camp, peopling.md section 4) links nothing, and the build reports it (Yang, 2026-10-20: until then a site only had to be nearer its own shore than the other, and with no site on Sulawesi both ends of the Wallace Line crossing lay in Borneo, and the Timor–Sahul crossing ran straight from Borneo to Australia; before 2026-10-19 both ends of the Korea–Japan crossing could fall in Korea). Wallacea is never joined to Asia or to Sahul on foot, at any sea level: the build checks it (section 7). From the crossing's date, a band crossing it walks to the shore, paddles along the crossing's way and walks on, as the scene already shows.
- **Stranded.** A band whose site is cut off by the rising sea, with no free site it can walk to, may paddle straight to the nearest site within 3,600 km (**Proposed**), putting in and landing wherever the way changes between land and water: to a free site, to a band there with dry places free for its lodgings, or, last of all, to any empty site it can use however near another band (the build counts these): people of the coasts had boats.
- **Paths.** Every move of a band is a path of links from site to site, worked out by the build and stored with the band's new lodging, so the scene walks it leg by leg and paddles where a leg crosses water.

## 4. How bands populate the sites

All of it is deterministic: whole days, the seeded hash and the build's own maths (humankind.md section 5).

- **Taken sites.** A site may hold one band. No two bands stand closer than the camp spacing of the density at the site (humankind.md section 4.1), so their drawn camps do not overlap.
- **Moving.** At the end of each residence window a band moves along one link of at most 400 km to a free usable site (it roams; Yang, 2026-10-19), the best by the habitat score of humankind.md section 4.3 (near water, open ground, low and green) with a little hash, or stays where no neighbour is free. A band none of whose home range is usable or free any more (the desert, ice or sea having taken it) first takes its home range anew from the sites usable now (Yang, 2026-10-20: "often camps move too rarely. there is one in sudan that didn't move from 48000 BCE to 8000 BCE"). A settled band stays.
- **The water comes.** When its site, or the lodging places it uses, will not stay dry until its next move, the band leaves first, to the nearest free usable site along links; failing that, it joins nearby bands with room, splitting up if it must (at most twenty people to a full camp, both its rings of spots, and eight to a compact one), or, last of all, takes any empty site it can use however near another band; failing that, it paddles away as a stranded band (section 3). Nobody is lost to the sea.
- **Budding.** A full camp sends two or three people to the best free usable site the rules allow, at least one camp spacing from every other band and at most five links away (**Proposed**).
- **Following a route** (planned legs; Yang, 2026-10-19: "can't you predefine which band needs to traverse which sites to reach japan?", after a change in Siberia left Japan unreached; since 2026-10-20 from the place table). Each leg of a route, from one arrival point to the next (peopling.md section 3), goes from its **origin**, the previous point's site in the place table (section 2.1), or none for a leg out of Africa, to its **target**, its own point's site. The leg is **reached** once a band stands at the target, or a band whose drawn camp would overlap the target's moves onto it, as for an anchor (section 4.1); where that band cannot move (held, settled, or more people than the target has room for), it stands for the target and the leg is reached at its camp. From the point's date, while it is not reached, the band at the origin, or failing that the band nearest the origin along links with people to spare, sends a group of two or three. It walks to the origin, then along the shortest path of open links from the origin to the target, across a listed crossing where the route crosses the sea, and founds a camp there. While no such path is open on the day (a land bridge under water), or for a leg without an origin, the band nearest the target along open links goes instead; while there is none, or the target is not usable that day, the leg waits and tries again. The day and the band's camp are what the events dialog shows for the point.
- **Band caps** (Yang, 2026-10-19: a hybrid of rules and a table, after the counts of bands in Siberia, South America and Australia kept shifting whenever any rule changed). A culture area may cap its bands: `maxBands` in its record, a list of `[bce, count]` pairs, each holding from its year until the next. A band does not bud into a site of a full area, and a band fleeing the sea or the desert goes to an area with room, or joins a band nearby, before it moves into a full one. Bands placed by routes (below) and anchors (section 4.1) count towards the cap but are placed even when the area is full, since they are history. Siberia has 1 (with the bands its route places at its three arrival points, about 3), Beringia east of the date line 1, Australia with Tasmania 3 and South America 10 (**Proposed**, from Yang's counts of 2026-10-19).
- **Home ranges.** When a band is founded it takes the site it founds and up to three usable sites linked to it within the roaming distance of 400 km, nearest first, that no other band holds: its home range. It roams only among them, and no other band buds onto them, so a band's comings and goings do not push others around. A band that must flee the sea (above), and a band that a route or anchor moves, takes a new home range where it lands; the sites of its old one become free.
- **The migration table.** The routes of the arrival map (peopling.md section 4, `data/curated/arrivals.json`) are the table of high-level migrations: who goes where and when, from point to point in order. A route keeps its own band: the band founded by a leg sends the group of the next leg, if it still stands and has people to spare; otherwise the band at the leg's origin, then the band nearest it, as below.
- **Rules bound to region and time.** Which sites may be used, at what density, by which culture and with which limits, comes from the rule records: the culture areas (humankind.md section 5, peopling.md section 5), the arrival map (peopling.md section 4), the Neolithic thresholds (peopling.md section 3.6), and morphs (section 5). They are the only place where history is put into the tree; the build has no other special cases.

### 4.1 Anchors

Later milestones need a band at an exact place and time: the camp that becomes the first village at the Nile before the Egyptians, or the band at the Palatine hill when Rome is founded (Yang, 2026-10-19: "I need to be able to specify exact time and position at which a site needs to have a band so that they can morph into a civilization like Egypt, or when Rome is founded"). An **anchor** in `data/curated/anchors.json` names one:

| Field | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `id` | The anchor, also used by the records that need it (a morph, a settlement) |
| `bce` | The year from which a band must stand there (or `ce`) |
| `lat`, `lon` | The place: the anchor's site is the camp site nearest it, within 300 km, that a camp can use on that day (dry and habitable for the culture there) |
| `until` | Optional: the year to which the band stays; without it, to the end of the tree |
| `scene` | Optional: a scene its band plays at its camp, a cave painting, a burial or a monument ([humankind.md](humankind.md#82-scenes-at-anchors) section 8.2) |
| `camp` | Optional: a `lat` and `lon` where its camp should stand, near the place, for a scene that faces the place from there (humankind.md section 8.2); its site is laid and chosen there |
| `en`, `de`, `source` | What it is, in both languages, and the source of its date and place |

The build makes it so:

- **The site.** Each anchor is a dated place of the place table (section 2.1): a site is laid near it, within 75 km where any layout fits (else as near as a key place's), dry for its span, and its site is the one the table chooses; without one the build fails naming the anchor.
- **Getting there.** The planning pass (section 4.2) plans it before the tree grows: it keeps the site free for the anchor from 500 years before the day (**Proposed**: long enough to walk there from a neighbouring region) and brings a band there, by stronger means the nearer the day comes.
- **Staying.** From its arrival until `until`, the band at the anchor's site is settled: it does not roam or bud, and its home range is the site alone. It still flees the sea; an anchor whose site the sea would flood within its span fails the build.
- **Checked.** The planning pass fails the build before the tree grows when an anchor cannot be met (section 4.2). If, all the same, no band stands at its site on its day, the build fails naming the anchor and the nearest band, so a wrong date or place shows at once. The checks of section 7 confirm that the band stays until `until`.
- The tree runs to 1991 CE (peopling.md section 3.5), so an anchor may have any date. The first anchor is Göbekli Tepe, where hunter-gatherers raised the stone enclosures from about 9500 BCE (Schmidt 2010, *Göbekli Tepe: the Stone Age sanctuaries*, Documenta Praehistorica 37),.

### 4.2 The planning pass

Yang, 2026-10-20: "let's build the planning pass so that we have a very robust way to resolve anchor event constraints". Until then an anchor was met as the tree grew, by whatever band happened to be near: other bands could roam, bud or flee into its site's way while its band was on the way, and a band driven off by the desert or ice failed the build in the middle of its stay. There will be hundreds of such records (anchors, the Neolithic thresholds, the events of scenes and, in milestone 3, morphs into settlements), so each is planned once, before the tree grows, from the geography and the dates alone, and the plan is then kept to.

- **Plans.** Each anchor and each Neolithic threshold (whose band is held as an anchor's, to the tree's end, peopling.md section 3.6) gets a plan: its site from the place table (section 2.1); its **claim**, from 500 years before its day (its **lead**) to its `until`; and its **footprint**, every site whose drawn camp would overlap its site's (section 2).
- **Checked before the tree grows.** The build fails, naming the record and the reason, when a plan cannot be met:
  - **Usable for the whole stay.** The site has room (its core and two lodging places, one for a compact camp, above the sea) and its hearth is livable for the culture there (no ice, no desert, not too cold or high, peopling.md section 5.2) at its day and every century to its `until`, so the held band is never driven off.
  - **Reached by people in time.** The arrival map reaches the site by the anchor's day, and on that day the site is joined, by at most 80 links open then, to a site the arrival map has reached by the start of the lead: land where bands can be.
  - **No clash.** Two plans whose claims overlap in time may not have different sites whose camps overlap. Plans on the same site share its band, held to the later `until`.
- **Kept free.** During its claim, the plan's site and footprint are kept for it. No other band roams, buds, flees, takes a home range or founds a camp there, and a stranded band does not land there; a route's leg whose target lies in the footprint goes to the plan's site instead, so its group meets the plan. When the claim begins, a band standing in the footprint moves onto the site if it fits (it becomes the plan's band, as before); any other makes way to the nearest free site, or, failing that, as a band fleeing the sea does (section 4: joining, paddling, crowding).
- **Delivery.** At each step of the claim until a band stands at the site, by stronger means as the day comes nearer: from the start of the lead, a group of two or three from the band nearest along open links with people to spare, as before; from halfway through the lead, also from a settled band (near a Neolithic threshold); in the last 100 years before the day, the nearest band that is not held, whole, along the shortest open path. As nobody else may enter the footprint, nothing stands in the way.
- **The table.** Each plan's claim, the day a band reached its site and how (already there, moved onto it, a group, a whole band) are written beside the record's row in `places.json` (section 2.1), and the build logs how many plans were met by each means.

### 4.3 Encounters

Yang, 2026-10-20: "In the future we will need interaction between multiple bands at the same or overlapping sites. E.g. wars between two nations, or trade routes, or visits, etc." and, on the design: "Let's spec and implement the infrastructure but dont do tepe yet". A band still lives at one site, alone (section 4). Everything bands do together is an **encounter**: parties from several bands travel to one place, stay there a while together and go home. Visits, feasts and gatherings, trade journeys and, later, the armies of a war are encounters; what follows from one (goods, a changed border, a morph) is for the milestones that need it. `data/curated/encounters.json` lists them; it is empty: the gathering at Göbekli Tepe (issue 132) was tried as an encounter, but its far guests were drawn appearing from nowhere (Yang, 2026-10-21), so that monument's visitors are instead the people of the camps round it, who walk there from their own lodgings (humankind.md section 8.2). A role never takes people from a band another role of the encounter has already sent people from, and the band of an encounter's host is kept to one lodging, so there is room at its site for the guests' lodgings.

| Field | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `id` | The encounter |
| `kind` | What it is, for the scene and the events dialog: `visit`, `gathering`, `trade` or `battle` (**Proposed**) |
| `bce` (or `ce`) | The year of its first occurrence |
| `every`, `until` | Optional: it happens again every `every` years, to the year `until` |
| `stay` | How long the parties stay together, in seconds of watching (**Proposed** default 60) |
| `lat`, `lon` | Where they meet: its site is a dated place of the place table (section 2.1), for every occurrence |
| `host` | Optional: the id of an anchor (section 4.1) whose band receives the parties at its camp; without it they meet on open ground, a **meeting site** with no band of its own |
| `roles` | The parties, at least one (two without a host): each with an `id`, the place it comes from (`lat`, `lon`, `km`: a band standing within that distance of it) and how many people come (`people`, two to eight). Bands stand far apart (a drawn camp covers hundreds of kilometres, and in the deep past few bands live at all: 48 worldwide about 32,000 BCE), and a meeting site's footprint is kept free, so a role's place should reach well beyond it: 1,500 to 2,500 km (**Proposed**; a trial in the Rift Valley met five of six occurrences with 2,000 km) |
| `en`, `de`, `source` | What it is, in both languages, and its source |

The build makes it so, as part of the planning pass (section 4.2):

- **Planned.** Each occurrence is a plan with several roles. Its meeting site is chosen by the place table and checked before the tree grows like an anchor's: it has room for the parties' lodgings (one for every four people, at most five lodgings and twenty people in all, with the host's own) and is livable at the occurrence; the arrival map reaches it and each role's place by the occurrence's day; and on that day each role's place is joined to the site by open links. A meeting site's footprint is kept free (section 4.2) from the occurrence's day to the parties' leaving; a host's is its anchor's. Occurrences may not clash with each other or with other plans (section 4.2), except a host's occurrences with its own anchor.
- **Going.** On the occurrence's day each role's party gathers from the bands standing within the role's place that are not held at a plan, nearest the site along open links first, each sending whom it can spare (all but one, never the first person), until the party is whole: bands of three to five are small, and several bands sending people to a feast is how it was. Each person walks the shortest open path of links from their own band, paddling where a link is a canoe crossing, as a band moving camp does. At a host, each party builds its own lodgings at free dry places of the host's camp; on a meeting site, the first party founds a camp of the encounter, which neither roams, buds nor grows, and the others build their lodgings at it. Each lodging is its party's culture's own, and its people wear their own clothes.
- **Staying.** For the stay the guests live at the camp as its own people do, in the camp life of humankind.md section 8.1.
- **Going home.** When the stay is over each party's people walk back, along the shortest open path, each to the band they came from (those born there with the party's first), wherever that band stands by then (or, if it is gone, the nearest band of its culture): into a lodging of its own there if a dry place is free, else into its band's lodgings with room, else, last of all, it founds a camp of its own at the nearest free usable site. An encounter's camp ends when its last party leaves.
- **The way.** A figure's walk to its next home normally follows the way stored with that lodging, or goes straight from camp to camp (section 3). A trip's walk goes along the trip's own path, which the figure keeps beside its homes as a **walk**: the day and lodging of the home it walks to, and the way (humankind.md section 3.1).
- **Failing.** An occurrence that a role cannot meet on its day (too few people to spare within its place, no open path) fails the build, naming the encounter, the role and the reason, as an anchor does. The build logs how many occurrences were met and how many people took part.
- **Tests.** On the synthetic world: two parties meet on open ground, stay together and go back to their bands; parties visit a host's camp and leave it as it was; a role with no band near fails before the tree grows; every trip walks along links only.

## 5. Morphs

A **morph** is a rule that, from a date, the bands in a region take on another culture: hunter-gatherers of the Levant becoming Natufians about 12,500 BCE, or, in milestone 3, foragers of the Nile valley becoming the farmers and then the towns of predynastic and dynastic Egypt. A culture area that starts on a date where another one was is a morph. Each band in the region changes once, at a moment chosen by hash within the morph's transition window (100 years, **Proposed**, as spec section 9 has eras morph gradually): it rebuilds its lodgings in the new culture's kind where it stands, and its people go into their huts and come out in the new clothes (peopling.md section 5.3). A band moving into a region with another culture changes the same way when it arrives. Morphs into settlements (towns, palaces, forts) come with milestone 3 and use the same records.

## 6. Foliage

Trees and camps share the ground (humankind.md section 7, [foliage.md](foliage.md) section 4), and the trees are planned with the bands (Yang, 2026-10-21: "for trees, can we use the same planning step as we do for bands and sites? I want to avoid bands setting up camp and the trees all falling to make space ... the trees die off slightly before a settlement is built"). The tree of humankind is built in full before anything is drawn, so when and where every band will camp is known ahead: the trees of a site thin out and wither over the 10 seconds of watching before its band arrives, so the band comes to open ground and fells nothing. While a band stands at a site the trees stay away, and when it leaves they grow back over the regrowth time (five years, about 12 seconds of watching), unless another band is coming there before they are grown: then the regrowth and the thinning before it meet, and the trees never stand fully again. A camp's clearing and the forest around it always agree, whichever band comes next. Each time window's file carries the lodgings built in the 10 seconds after it, so the thinning begins in the window before.

## 7. Build, tests and tools

- **Stage.** `data/build/stages/sites.ts` (**Proposed**) builds the sites and links after the climate stage and before the tree, writes them to `data/build/tree/sites.json` (git-ignored) for the tools, and logs how many sites and links there are and how many are usable at a few dates. The sites take about a minute to lay out, so the build keeps them in `data/out/sites/` (git-ignored) by a hash of everything they depend on: the source data, the history lens, the key places, the crossings, the coarser lattices of the culture areas, the camp layouts and the build code that makes the land and the sites. Tuning the tree (densities, spacings, routes, morphs) then rebuilds only the tree. Only the newest set is kept.
- **Checks** (failing the build): every lodging stands at a usable site and place on the day it is built and until it is taken down; every walked leg of a band's path is a link usable on the day it is walked; no two bands stand closer than their spacing; no band other than a plan's own stands in its footprint during its claim (section 4.2), from the end of the step that cleared it (a band joining others leaves at the end of the step).
- **Tests.** Unit tests on a small synthetic world: a site whose core floods is not used; a band on a shrinking island leaves before the water reaches it; a land bridge link opens and closes with the sea; a morph changes each band once, inside its hut; a band returning to a site fells only what has regrown. For the planning pass: an anchor is met with every band capped far away; a big camp on the site's footprint makes way; an anchor whose site turns to desert within its stay, one never reached by people and two clashing anchors fail before the tree grows, naming the reason; two anchors on one site share its band. The water test on the full data (milestone 1 section 9) keeps checking every figure, prey and lodging the scene shows.
- **Development view** (Yang, 2026-10-19: "add a debug option to show the lattice"). With `?sites` in the address, the globe shows every site as a dot, green for a full camp, yellow for a compact one, orange for a minimal one and pale for a waypoint, dimmed to dark blue while the sea at the clock's date covers it, and every link as a line, bright while it can be walked or paddled. The build writes the sites and links for it as one small file, which the app loads only with the flag.
